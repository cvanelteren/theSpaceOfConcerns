\newcommand{\significancetext}{%
    \textbf{The Problem} Protecting global commons like Antarctica requires consensus among diverse nations. However, because consensus regimes rely on unanimity, formal voting records do not exist. This makes it difficult to understand how national priorities emerge, how agendas expand, or why certain crucial governance issues stall.

    \vspace{1em}

    \textbf{The Findings} We introduce the ``space of concerns'': a network built from 64 years of Antarctic governance documents, which links issues that attract specialization by the same actors. The space has a dense procedural--environmental core with bounded branches, and actors occupy distinct positions within it that track how long they have been active. Portfolios then grow along the network rather than across it. States rarely leap into unfamiliar domains, building instead on what lies adjacent to existing capability. Actors with broader and more strongly anchored portfolios reach emerging concerns earlier, an association we report descriptively rather than causally.

    \vspace{1em}

    \textbf{The Impact} As environmental pressures, tourism, and resource interest in Antarctica intensify, an institution whose agenda expands only along existing portfolios risks responding slowly to new ones. The space of concerns makes unequal agenda capacity observable before final decisions are taken, letting commons managers and diplomats identify structural bottlenecks and test mechanisms for more equitable participation.

\begin{abstract}
% --- ABSTRACT (single paragraph, ~150 words, no citations) ---
\AbstractLettrine{C}{onsensus governance leaves no roll calls, so it is hard to see which Parties can put an issue on the agenda and which cannot. The documents themselves still show it. Analysing 6,573 papers submitted to the Antarctic Treaty by 66 actors between 1961 and 2025, we link policy topics that attract disproportionate engagement from the same actors, giving a ``space of concerns'' in which every actor holds a position. Three results follow. First, the space is organized, with a dense procedural--environmental core and bounded branches toward tourism, resources and strategic planning; and it is not a restatement of what topics are called, since label wording explains under $2\%$ of it. Second, portfolios grow along the network rather than across it: each $0.1$ increase in distance from an actor's existing portfolio multiplies the odds of taking up a concern by $0.75$, an estimate that survives five ways of building the space, and a simple retain-and-adopt rule reproduces the record's breadth, topic popularity and local entry. Third, actors with broader and more anchored portfolios reach emerging concerns earlier. Because issues are taken up through adjacent ones, the practical lever is adjacency: helping a Party connect a new concern to one it already sustains should widen participation more than general capacity-building.}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Global environmental governance is usually studied through its rules, including the treaties signed, protocols ratified, and measures adopted. Yet a rule is the end of a process that starts much earlier, when some problems begin attracting sustained attention and others do not. By the time a decision is written down, an institution has already settled which concerns it will take up and which it will leave at the margin \cite{BachrachBaratz1962,Kingdon1984}. We observe that earlier sorting process much less well.

Two difficulties compound one another because institutions of this kind are legible mainly to people who already know them. Reading a submission record requires knowing which agenda item a phrase attaches to, which subsidiary body it will travel to, and which delegations have carried it before. Close-range studies recover that procedural context by concentrating on a single negotiation or period \cite{Joyner1998,Dodds2019,Drubel2023HiddenContestation}, but they do not readily aggregate across sixty years or transfer to another institution. Consensus creates a second problem because unanimity produces no roll calls, removing the usual quantitative shortcut for recovering positions \cite{Steinberg2002,Stone2013,Abbott2015}. Both problems have the same substantive root. The procedural fluency an outside analyst lacks is also what a state needs to place a concern on the agenda. Difficulty of study and difficulty of entry are one barrier seen from two sides.

Where the study of agendas has scaled, it has scaled by counting. Large-$N$ agenda research measures how much attention issues receive and how abruptly priorities shift \cite{BaumgartnerJones1993,JonesBaumgartner2005,GreenPedersenWalgrave2014}; text-as-data work recovers state positions from repeated international records at a scale no case study can reach \cite{Baturo2017}. The Antarctic record has been read this way too, and productively: submission counts per Party have been used to identify a small leadership group that carries most of the substantive papers \cite{DudeneyWalton2012}, to describe the resulting participation asymmetry across thematic areas \cite{Sanchez2016}, and to measure the throughput of Treaty decision-making itself \cite{Gardiner2025}. What counting yields is a quantity per actor or per topic, and that is also its ceiling. A count says how much attention a concern received, and who supplied it. It cannot say which concerns are held together, how an actor's concerns sit relative to one another, or where an actor could credibly go next.

\beginarticlecolumns

What counts omit is the relation among concerns: how they sit relative to one another, and where actors stand once they are arranged. Two actors can share almost no specialized topics and still occupy neighbouring positions, because each works next to what the other does (Supplementary Information \cref{fig:country-overlap-vs-phi}). Counting recovers common ground; co-specialization recovers specialist corridors. The two are differently blind rather than better and worse, and the trade-off is set out in the Methods; our object here is the second, because it is what supplies positions, neighbourhoods and directions of movement where counting yields only a magnitude.

That relational structure can be represented directly, as a network whose nodes are the concerns themselves. Two concerns are joined when the same actors work on both more than usual; an actor then occupies a neighbourhood rather than a list, and moving to an adjacent concern is easier than moving to a distant one, so where an actor can go next depends on where it already sits. We call this network the \emph{space of concerns}. An actor holds a \emph{Revealed Policy Advantage} in a topic when it devotes a disproportionate share of its submissions to that topic; two topics are proximate when actors advantaged in one are typically advantaged in the other. Because the network is estimated from the record the institution keeps under its own rules, it shows how decision-making is prepared within those rules, showing which concerns actors sustain, what lies adjacent to them, and where each actor could credibly go next. Economic complexity offers the closest methodological precedent, inferring relatedness among products from co-specialization \cite{Hidalgo2007,Hidalgo2018}, and expansion into related activities has been documented across products, industries, regions, and research fields \cite{Neffke2011,Boschma2017,Guevara2016}. Here the object is governance rather than growth, and in a consensus institution the relational structure of attention is where unequal position becomes observable. We build the space for the Antarctic Treaty System, the longest-running commons regime with a complete documentary record.

Since 1959 that regime has demilitarized a continent, suspended sovereignty claims, and taken its decisions by consensus \cite{AntarcticTreaty1959,Joyner1998,Yao2021}; Antarctica and the Southern Ocean are also an iconic global commons whose rapid environmental change carries planetary consequences \cite{Kennicutt2019,Sampaio2022}. Our corpus is 6,573 working papers and information documents submitted to the Antarctic Treaty Consultative Meetings between 1961 and 2025, from 66 actors across 45 topics (Supplementary Information \cref{fig:topic-submission-overview}; Methods). The topic labels are the Treaty Secretariat's own, so the recovered structure maps how actors make concerns legible to the institution rather than how an outside coder would group them \cite{Yaryab2024,Gardiner2025}. Three scope conditions hold throughout. The unit of analysis is the submitting actor, meaning consultative parties, non-consultative parties, observers, expert bodies, and the Secretariat, rather than formal decision authority. \emph{Agenda capacity} means the ability to keep issues present in the record, connect them to established procedure, and move into related concerns. And \emph{agenda} means the document-system agenda; nothing that follows speaks to control over final consensus outcomes. Three engagement modes---coordination and exchange, compliance and management, and strategy and resources---summarize actor positions as contiguous regions of the recovered space (Methods). They are regions of the issue topology rather than blocs of actors, and they let the map and the actor analysis share one vocabulary from the outset.

The empirical argument moves from the topology itself to actor position, portfolio change, and earlier entry. The space has a dense procedural--environmental core with narrow branches toward tourism, resources, and strategic planning, while its network properties show a connected but bounded topology without a single structural anchor. Actors occupy distinct parts of that topology, and those positions change slowly. Across actor windows, 82.0\% of transitions remain in the same region and 99.0\% remain in the same or an adjacent one. Locality also appears in portfolio growth. Across 1,055 choice sets and 40,129 at-risk observations, each $0.1$ increase in distance from an actor's existing portfolio multiplies the odds of entry by $0.75$ ($\beta_{\mathrm{distance}}=-2.93$). Topics persist once adopted, and a retain-and-adopt rule reproduces the breadth, popularity, and local-entry pattern of the record. Broader and more strongly anchored portfolios are associated with earlier entry into emerging concerns, although that association is descriptive. Together these results identify a constraint that is specific enough to act on. Because concerns are taken up through adjacent ones, an actor's reach is set by what it already sustains, and the effective way to widen participation is to shorten the distance between an emerging concern and an established one rather than to raise general capacity. That is a narrower and more testable target than the usual prescriptions, and \cref{sec:lowering-barriers} sets out what a trial of it would look like.

\section{Results}
\subsection{The Space of Concerns Has a Core, Branches, and No Single Anchor}
Rather than a loose topical cloud, the space of concerns has a dense procedural--environmental core and bounded branches. In \cref{fig:the_space_of_concerns}, topic proximity is estimated from repeated cross-actor co-specialization, so distance reflects recurrent institutional co-engagement rather than semantic similarity. That holds by construction, since no edge is computed from text. Whether the resulting geometry nonetheless coincides with the wording of the labels is a separate question, and one worth settling rather than assuming: actors might well specialize in topics that are also semantically alike. They do not. Compared against a TF-IDF cosine over the same 45 Secretariat labels, lexical similarity explains $1.2\%$ of the variance in proximity for word $n$-grams and $1.8\%$ for character $n$-grams (Mantel permutation $p=0.002$ and $p=0.001$; Methods). The strongest ties in the map join labels with no shared vocabulary at all---Exchange of information with Opening statements ($\phi=0.52$), Environmental protection with Liability ($\phi=0.48$), Inspections with Waste management and disposal ($\phi=0.47$)---while some of the most lexically similar pairs sit far apart, as when Comprehensive Environmental Evaluations and the State of the Antarctic Environment Report share much of their wording but almost no constituency ($\phi=0.07$). At the center sits a core built around opening statements, exchange of information, environmental monitoring and reporting, environmental protection, liability, and inspections; from it, thinner branches run toward resource and science frontiers, tourism and visitation, and operational coordination. Node colours anticipate the three engagement modes in which actors organize themselves (\cref{fig:portfolio_space_ridgelines}), and each mode occupies a contiguous stretch of the topology, so the map and the actor analysis share one vocabulary from the first page. Terms such as ``core'', ``corridor'', and ``chain'' describe visually dense or elongated parts of the network; they are not separately estimated clusters.

Some links mainly validate the recovered topology. The close tie between Waste management and disposal and Inspections shows that environmental management and compliance monitoring are coupled in practice, embedding apparently technical concerns in compliance and verification pathways. Two Treaty constraints also shape the geometry. The 1959 Treaty demilitarized the continent and reserved it for peaceful purposes \cite{AntarcticTreaty1959,Joyner1998,Yao2021}, and the 1991 Madrid Protocol prohibited mineral resource activities while allowing tightly regulated scientific drilling \cite{MadridProtocol1991,Koivurova2005}; accordingly, a resource-and-science corridor links management and assessment to environmental classification, marine acoustics, drilling, and sub-glacial lakes---an adjacency where scientific value and potential strategic relevance coexist, without the topology establishing actors' motives or implying that science is disguised conflict \cite{Sampaio2022,HansenMagnusson2022,DoddsBoulegue2023,Szpak2025}. Access pressures enter the agenda through conservation and safety obligations rather than overt distributive bargaining, with inspections as a compliance hinge and wilderness and marine protected areas bridging the core to tourism, pollution prevention, and search and rescue \cite{Haward2012,Goldsworthy2022,Meyer2022,Luo2024AntarcticTourism}. One less obvious chain runs from the operation of the environmental committee through the State of the Antarctic Environment Report to Environmental Domains Analysis; the chain is not merely correlated concerns but an institutional route connecting procedure, assessment, and the spatial classification of environmental knowledge \cite{Haas1992}.

Relatedness also extends beyond direct edges. The recovered geometry embeds shortest-path distances on the topic graph (Methods), so two concerns with little direct co-specialization can still sit close when they share strong intermediaries. Because single-step entry is local (\cref{fig:hazard-distance-timeseries}), mediated proximity marks the route through which influence on a distant concern would travel---actors reach far concerns by stepping along the corridor rather than leaping to them. The map shows the institutional route through which attention would have to move; it does not estimate influence itself.

The network's global properties establish what kind of terrain this geometry represents. With only 37 of 990 topic pairs at zero proximity (3.74\%) and no fully isolated topic, the space is broadly connected but bounded; the zero ties concentrate among peripheral technical and environmental concerns (Supplementary Information \cref{fig:heatmap_concerns}), so the core is a relative concentration of strong ties within a connected network, not a separation into disconnected clusters. Across time, link density, giant-component size, and closeness rise as participation expands, particularly after the entry of new members in the 1990s, while clustering remains low and betweenness stays low and stable (Supplementary Information \cref{fig:space_network_metrics}), and no single topic controls passage between parts of the agenda. Greedy actor-removal tests produce gradual rather than abrupt changes through most of the removal sequence (Supplementary Information \cref{fig:sensitivity-analysis}), so the topology is jointly sustained rather than inherited from one dominant contributor. The resulting space permits movement but makes some moves more difficult than others; because no single node or actor organizes it, position has to be studied within the network.

\subsection{Where Actors Sit in the Space}
Actor portfolios concentrate on limited stretches of the topology rather than scattering across it (\cref{fig:portfolio_space_ridgelines}A). Position tracks tenure, with earlier entrants sitting further along the axis (Spearman $\rho=-0.58$, $p=9\times10^{-6}$), although position is also strongly tied to breadth ($\rho=0.74$), so tenure and capacity cannot be disentangled here. We summarize these positions with three recurring modes along that gradient, namely coordination and exchange (scientific integration, education, information exchange), compliance and management (inspections, liability, protected areas), and strategy and resources (mineral resources, tourism pressure, multiyear planning); long-standing members such as the United States, Russia, Australia, and New Zealand are prominent in this third region (Supplementary Information \cref{fig:regime-tenure}). That group largely coincides with the leadership set recovered from raw submission counts over a shorter window \cite{DudeneyWalton2012}, which is a useful check on the actor dimension specifically. The two measures are differently blind in general, but they are expected to agree about membership here, because at the high-volume end volume and specialization co-move: an actor submitting many papers across a narrow set of topics scores highly on both. Their divergence lies elsewhere, among actors whose few submissions are concentrated enough to register as specialization, and on topics of near-universal participation, where counting registers a link and co-specialization cannot. What the relational view adds is therefore not the membership of that group but its position---which concerns it holds, what lies adjacent to them, and what the rest of the system therefore has to reach across. The pattern follows this internal gradient more closely than external geopolitical blocs. Standard groupings such as the EU, NATO, or BRICS, and geographic distance, do not recover it (Supplementary Information \cref{fig:actors_on_space,fig:geo-portfolio-overlap,fig:bloc-overlap}).

The modes are regions of the issue topology, not clusters of actors. Operationally, we order topics along the recovered space, locate three zones where excess engagement concentrates, and assign each topic to the nearest zone center (Supplementary Information \cref{fig:regime-k-validation}; Supplementary Information Tables~\ref{tab:regime_topics_full} and~\ref{tab:regime_alignment_full}). Two limits should be stated plainly. The ordering axis carries 23.8\% of the eigenvalue mass of the topic distance matrix and shows no eigengap at three clusters (Methods), and $k=3$ is selected by an elbow on a smooth inertia curve, so the partition is an interpretive division of a continuum rather than a discovery of three groups. Because the partition is defined on that axis, the zone bands in \cref{fig:portfolio_space_ridgelines}A describe how actor mass distributes along the ordering, not independent evidence that three modes exist. Every statistic stated in modes inherits that caveat, which is why the central locality result (\cref{fig:hazard-distance-timeseries}) is computed without any partition. The two summaries are not redundant, however. An actor's dominant mode is the single largest share of its portfolio mass, whereas its plotted position is the RPA-weighted mean of that mass, and the two disagree for 12 of the 50 actors in \cref{fig:portfolio_space_ridgelines}A (24.0\%), including France, the United Kingdom, the United States and Australia, whose mean position falls in the compliance zone while their modal mass sits in strategy and resources. Marker colour in \cref{fig:portfolio_space_ridgelines}A is therefore not a recoding of the horizontal axis. A further asymmetry is visible in the same panel: no actor's mean position exceeds $0.61$ of the axis, so even the most strategy-oriented portfolios are pulled back toward the procedural core, and the outer stretch of the space is worked topic by topic without any actor centring there. The partition-free view also corrects a common misreading of overlap, in which many sovereign-state pairs share few specialized topics yet occupy adjacent, mutually supportive regions (\cref{fig:portfolio_space_ridgelines}B), a pattern of selective complementarity around a shared core rather than duplication of focus.

\subsection{Portfolio Evolution Is Local}
Position is not a fixed attribute of an actor, but it changes slowly and locally. The cleanest statement of this requires no partition at all. For each actor and each pair of adjacent rolling 5-year windows, we compare where the actor's newly specialized topics actually sit relative to its prior portfolio against a size-matched redraw from the topics that actor did not already hold, so breadth, era, and activity volume cancel within the pair. Of the 1,055 expansion events in the record (1,895 individual topic moves), 83.0\% land nearer than their own null (median shift $-0.068$ in $1-\max\phi$; Wilcoxon $p<10^{-100}$), and a further 46.9\% of actor-transitions add no new specialization at all. Stated instead in modes, 82.0\% of transitions remain in the same mode, 99.0\% remain in the same or an adjacent mode, and direct Mode 1$\leftrightarrow$Mode 3 jumps are rare (1.0\%, spread across 9 actors with no systematic pattern; \cref{fig:hazard-distance-timeseries}A); that version is easier to read, which is why the partition-free paired test rather than the mode percentages carries the claim. A direct 1-year versus 5-year comparison (Supplementary Information \cref{fig:regime-window-sensitivity}) shows the expected smoothing, with pooled same-mode persistence rising from 58.6\% to 82.0\% while pooled direct Mode 1$\leftrightarrow$Mode 3 jumps fall from 2.7\% to 1.0\%. Movement therefore happens, but it proceeds through the middle of the space rather than across it. Plotting where each of the 1,895 moves started against where it ended puts their mass along the diagonal, with no second population of long-range moves behind the average (\cref{fig:hazard-distance-timeseries}B). These percentages are different estimands of one locality claim; Supplementary Information Table~\ref{tab:locality_estimands} consolidates them.

Individual histories show what that gradual movement looks like (Supplementary Information \cref{fig:actor-trajectories}). Australia, active since the first Consultative Meeting, cycles between coordination and compliance work for three decades before settling into a mix weighted toward compliance and frontier topics. The Netherlands enters in 1991 with most of its mass in coordination and shifts steadily toward compliance. Ukraine enters in 2002 almost entirely in coordination, stays there for over a decade, and then moves sharply into compliance and frontier work after 2017. These are slow reallocations within a shared space, not jumps between fixed categories---the modes are best understood as constrained orientations. If position is acquired rather than assigned, the archive should show the process by which actors acquire it.

Portfolio expansion is strongly local. Actors are substantially more likely to specialize in topics near their existing portfolios than in distant ones, even when distance is measured in a cumulative-lagged topology built only from prior information, so the topology predicts where actors move next rather than merely describing them. If agenda capacity reflects latent capabilities that are partly organized through institutional pathways, actors should not expand through the recovered topology at random; new specialization should appear where existing expertise and institutional position already provide footholds. The hazard analysis treats growth as actor-specific choices between adjacent 5-year windows (Methods). Topics with above-average engagement (RPA $\ge 1$) form the prior portfolio, the remaining topics form the at-risk set, and each candidate is assigned a distance to the prior portfolio, one minus its strongest proximity to any held topic. The test asks whether, among the topics available to an actor at a given time, the nearer ones are more likely to be newly adopted.

Across 1,055 actor-period choice sets (40,129 at-risk topic observations), the cumulative-lagged estimate strongly supports local portfolio expansion. Raw distance in the space of concerns, $d_{ait}=1-\max_{j\in S_{a,t-1}}\phi(i,j)$, enters strongly negatively. A $0.1$ increase in distance multiplies the odds that an at-risk topic enters the portfolio, relative to the other at-risk topics in that actor-period, by $0.75$ (95\% CI $[0.72, 0.77]$; $\beta_{\mathrm{distance}}=-2.93$, 95\% CI $[-3.27, -2.59]$), net of a positive control for prior topic popularity. Panel C of \cref{fig:hazard-distance-timeseries} shows the same pattern nonparametrically on the same raw distance scale, with specialization-entry highest in the nearest distance bins and falling to near zero in the farthest bins. The pattern is stronger in the pooled full-history space and remains visible in the more volatile instantaneous previous-window space (Supplementary Information \cref{fig:hazard-space-sensitivity}). It also survives two stricter constructions of the space itself, namely recomputing each actor's distances from a space estimated without that actor's own submissions ($\beta_{\mathrm{distance}}=-2.57$) and weighting co-sponsored papers fractionally across sponsors ($\beta_{\mathrm{distance}}=-2.84$; Methods). The second of these is the more demanding test, because dropping a single actor barely perturbs the geometry ($r=0.99$), whereas fractional counting rebuilds the proximity matrix appreciably ($r=0.84$) and the estimate still holds. \cref{fig:hazard-distance-timeseries}D places all five specifications on one axis, in odds-ratio units. We therefore interpret local adoption as a temporally predictive feature of the Treaty-centered archive rather than as an artifact of reconstructing the topology from the full historical record.

Locality is paired with persistence, as activity remains high across subsequent years under one- to five-gap tolerance definitions once a topic has been adopted (Supplementary Information \cref{fig:retention-curves}). The latent capabilities reflected in agenda capacity can therefore accumulate cumulatively because actors that enter a concern tend to remain available as carriers of that issue into adjacent debates. New attention tends to accumulate by extending an existing portfolio into neighboring concerns rather than by repeatedly abandoning one issue set for another.

\subsection{A Simple Local Retain-and-Adopt Model Recovers the Main Structure}
Locality and persistence together motivate a stronger test, asking whether a simple local process can recover the observed system without assuming broad diffusion across the topology. In the sequential retain-and-adopt model, actors retain part of their existing portfolio, adopt a small number of nearby topics, and allocate effort within that support (\cref{fig:retain-and-adopt}A; Methods), holding fixed the observed active actors in each year, their budgets, and the pooled geometry of the space.

The fitted model matches mean actor breadth closely (observed $3.35$, simulated $3.32$) and mean topic popularity almost exactly (observed $2.45$, simulated $2.45$), tracks their temporal variation ($r=0.87$ and $r=0.98$; Supplementary Information \cref{fig:split-support-breadth-popularity}), and preserves the local-entry pattern (observed mean entry rank $0.677$, simulated $0.675$; \cref{fig:retain-and-adopt}B). The alternatives each fail on something. Direct allocation can appear local only by inflating breadth, and single support keeps breadth but cannot reach the observed locality, so what distinguishes retain-and-adopt is matching breadth, popularity, and locality at once. Participation, budget formation, and possible changes in the space of concerns are held outside the model. Because the entry stage already contains a $\phi$-proximity term, this is a sufficiency result rather than an independent confirmation of locality. It is also not an artifact of the denser late-period archive: under repeated year-balanced thinning at 40--80\% retention of raw submissions, breadth-tracking correlations remain between $0.78$ and $0.86$ and the local-entry signature stays clearly above the random baseline (Supplementary Information \cref{fig:split-support-thinning}).

\subsection{Who Reaches Emerging Concerns First}
If expansion is bounded by adjacency, then what an actor already holds sets the range of what it can plausibly reach next, and the question of access becomes a question about portfolios rather than about blocs. Across the 64 actors with sufficient history, earliness of entry rises with portfolio breadth ($+2.95$ index points per standard deviation, 95\% CI $[1.19, 4.71]$, $p=0.001$) and with how strongly that portfolio is anchored in a single mode ($+3.11$ per standard deviation, 95\% CI $[1.57, 4.64]$, $p<0.001$), while the two mode contrasts are indistinguishable from zero (Compliance versus Coordination $-0.44$, $p=0.77$; Strategy versus Coordination $-0.52$, $p=0.81$; $R^2=0.24$; Supplementary Information \cref{fig:who-enters-first,fig:pioneer_plot}).

The two halves of that result matter jointly. Early access is not distributed by bloc---actors of all three orientations appear among both the earliest and the latest entrants---but it is not evenly distributed either. It tracks breadth and anchoring, precisely the properties that locality makes cumulative---an actor that already spans many neighboring concerns has more adjacent ground to move onto, and one that has consolidated a position has more of it within reach. The barrier to early participation is therefore structural rather than factional, which is a more tractable problem; portfolios can be built, whereas blocs must be joined. Because the estimates are cross-sectional and their predictors are entangled among long-established actors, they identify an association rather than a mechanism, and nothing in the association implies a time ordering among the terms.

\section{Discussion}
Formal unanimity compresses disagreement into official outcomes, which can make consensus institutions appear less political than they are. Much of the consequential positioning occurs earlier---through agenda-setting, issue linkage, evidence production, and selective engagement \cite{BachrachBaratz1962,Stone2013,Abbott2015,Steinberg2002,Drubel2023HiddenContestation}. Our claim concerns the visibility of that process, not actors' intentions. The space of concerns recovers part of it from revealed issue activity when votes and explicit position-taking are unavailable.

Treaty attention forms a high-dimensional topology with a dense procedural--environmental core and bounded paths toward tourism, strategic planning, and resource-related science. The network is broadly connected, but it has neither tight global clustering nor a single structural anchor. Actors occupy different parts of this topology, and their positions change gradually. New specialization tends to occur near an existing portfolio, while adopted concerns usually persist. A retain-and-adopt process built around those two features reproduces the coarse breadth and popularity of the record. The evidence therefore moves from structure to behavior rather than treating the map as a descriptive endpoint.

This sequence supports a narrower and more useful claim than control over final decisions. Sustained attention also does not imply agreement. The space records who keeps an issue present, not whether positions converge. Consensus relocates political structure upstream, into differences in which concerns actors can sustain and which adjacent concerns they can credibly enter. The association between earlier entry and broad, anchored portfolios is consistent with that interpretation, but it remains descriptive because breadth, anchoring, tenure, and activity volume cannot be cleanly separated in this sample. Earlier entrants also appear in every mode, so the result does not identify a single privileged bloc. Counting-based readings of the same archive reach a compatible but differently shaped conclusion, describing a small and stable group of Parties that supplies most substantive papers \cite{DudeneyWalton2012,Sanchez2016}. We do not dispute that concentration; the relational view locates it. Concentration in volume becomes, on the space of concerns, a position---a set of held concerns with particular neighbours---and it is the neighbourhood, not the volume, that determines which concerns lie one step away for whom. It identifies a shared arena with unequal positions---a view that complements work on governance for sustainability and networked forms of agency \cite{Agrawal2022,Rolfer2025b}.

These results describe a relational summary of joint specialization, not an external semantic ontology or a map that actors literally consult. Topics are nearby because actors repeatedly devote disproportionate attention to them together, so the geometry reflects both political behavior and the institution's own categories. The pooled space is a long-run summary---not a claim that issue relations remained fixed or that any actor knew the full topology at the time. Supplementary Information \cref{fig:decadal-space-stack} shows occupation and edge intensity changing across decades on a common layout, and Supplementary Information \cref{fig:space-ribbon-full} shows the actor-level support behind those ties shifting across windows. We therefore use the cumulative-lagged space for the temporal adoption test and reserve the pooled space for description and model benchmarking.

Actors overlap enough to remain in one consensus arena, but some strongly anchored state pairs cover different, structurally adjacent parts of it. We interpret this as selective complementarity around a shared core rather than a full division of labor (Supplementary Information \cref{fig:fuzzy-pair-coverage,fig:regime-pair-institutional,fig:regime-tenure}).

The approach should travel as a measurement strategy, not as an empirical regularity already established across consensus institutions. It is best suited to settings with repeated agenda documents, stable or harmonized issue categories, and enough recurring actors to estimate co-specialization. It is less useful when agenda-setting occurs mainly off record, labels change too quickly to align, or participation is sparse. Nor does it replace a model of formal consensus formation or regime effectiveness \cite{Young2011Effectiveness}. Antarctic Measures, Decisions, and Resolutions still depend on legal procedure, consultative status, drafting coalitions, timing, and negotiation. The concern space identifies the terrain on which those processes operate---it does not provide a mechanical path from specialization to adoption. Applications to other document-rich forums could test whether the Antarctic pattern travels. The United Nations General Debate corpus shows that repeated international records can recover otherwise hidden state positions \cite{Baturo2017}.

Formal statements and final decisions omit the slower reorganization of institutional attention. Repeated co-specialization provides a way to observe that process and extends product-space reasoning from economies to governance \cite{Hidalgo2007,Hidalgo2018}. Environmental change, scientific development, and geopolitical pressure are altering both the Antarctic policy environment and the role of expertise within it \cite{Chown2013,Solomon2016,Holland2020,Nature2018ReformATS,Turekian2025ScienceDiplomacy}. The results point to two practical questions, namely whether institutions can lower the cost of entering adjacent concerns, and whether transparency can keep strategically sensitive scientific capabilities legible without imputing intent.

\subsection*{Lowering barriers to agenda participation in the Antarctic commons}\label{sec:lowering-barriers}
Because actors tend to enter concerns through areas they already sustain, local portfolio growth turns unequal agenda capacity into a practical institutional problem. Participation in emerging issues may depend partly on access to observations, drafting expertise, and recognized procedural pathways. Among Consultative Parties, formally equal consensus rights coexist with different portfolio positions; other actors contribute to the documentary agenda without holding the same decision rights. The analysis does not show that information or drafting costs cause these differences; it identifies a mechanism that can be tested.

Intervention should therefore focus on the infrastructure through which observations become documents and documents become recognized concerns. Building on existing reporting support \cite{ATCMResolution4_2026}, a prospective pilot could combine joint monitoring, co-sponsored working papers, and technical assistance in document preparation, with pre-specified outcomes testing whether participants co-sponsor more papers, sustain concerns across windows, or enter adjacent topics earlier. Testing is timely as Treaty Parties develop a framework for tourism and non-governmental activities \cite{IAATO2026Vessel,ATCM48Outcomes2026}. Better information infrastructure will not democratize governance by itself, because its effects depend on who defines categories, controls access, and converts information into recognized interventions \cite{Kloppenburg2022}; the neighbouring fisheries commission has adopted no new Southern Ocean marine protected area since the Ross Sea designation in 2016, despite sustained attention to the question \cite{Brooks2019RossSea,CCAMLRMeasures2026}. Such measures would lower the cost of connecting a concern to an established pathway without changing formal decision rights, and are tests of whether barriers to agenda participation can be reduced rather than claims of systemic transformation \cite{Moallemi2025a}.

The second question concerns the resource-and-science corridor, where drilling, sub-glacial lakes, and marine acoustics have clear scientific purposes while the associated knowledge and infrastructure may also matter to future resource or strategic questions \cite{Sampaio2022,HansenMagnusson2022,Szpak2025}. The co-specialization pattern identifies that adjacency; it does not reveal motives, establish covert dual-use activity, or show science to be conflict in disguise. Transparency there is therefore a question for institutional assessment rather than a conclusion of this analysis. Existing reporting and inspection mechanisms offer starting points, but their legal and procedural fit would need to be established separately, and any proposal would need to apply the same criteria to comparable activities by all Parties. The map can identify where scrutiny may be useful; it cannot determine intent or substitute for legal and political analysis.

\subsection*{Conclusion}
Formal equality in decision rules can coexist with unequal capacity to sustain concerns. The space of concerns makes that difference observable before final decisions are taken. It shows where actors sit, which concerns lie within reach of their existing portfolios, and where institutional bottlenecks may arise. Shared monitoring, drafting support, and actor-neutral transparency are hypotheses for addressing those bottlenecks, not effects established by this study. Institutional adaptation depends on formal rights, but it also depends on who can connect an emerging problem to sustained institutional attention.

\subsection{Data Description and Processing}
We analyze working papers and information documents submitted to the Antarctic Treaty System (ATS) from 1961 to 2025. Throughout the paper, ``ATS'' denotes the broader Antarctic governance complex anchored in the Antarctic Treaty and related instruments, but the empirical corpus used here comes from the Treaty-centered document archive rather than the full documentary output of every ATS institution. The corpus was compiled from the official ATS document archive and enriched with thematic labels, submitting parties, and document types \cite{Yaryab2024}. Documents submitted by the ATS Secretariat were retained for completeness. The Secretariat therefore plays two roles in the corpus by assigning the harmonized category labels for all documents while also appearing as one submitting actor among the 66; the labels are applied uniformly across submitters, and Secretariat submissions enter the analysis on the same terms as any other actor.

Data collection was performed on 6 February 2026 from the ATS website (\url{https://www.ats.aq/devAS/Meetings/DocDatabase?lang=e}) using the antarctic-database-go collector by Carlo Hamalainen (\url{https://github.com/carlohamalainen/antarctic-database-go}). The framework supports reproducibility by storing cached Hypertext Transfer Protocol (HTTP) responses and local copies of retrieved source documents, recording processing status in SQLite, and enabling deterministic re-runs and audits of the extraction pipeline. If the website changes or documents are removed, the collected dataset can therefore be reconstructed from the cached responses, allowing the analysis to be replicated even as the source data evolves.

The category labels are Secretariat-assigned (not natural language processing (NLP)-derived) and thus the space reflects co-participation within the Secretariat's own issue taxonomy rather than semantic similarity. \subsection{Constructing the Space of Concerns}
Before the construction itself, it is worth stating precisely what this measure sees and what it cannot. Counting and co-specialization are differently blind rather than better and worse versions of one another. Where participation in a concern is nearly universal, counting registers a link but co-specialization cannot, because no actor is disproportionately active in it; conversely, where a small community concentrates on a few technical concerns, co-specialization registers a link even though raw overlap stays low. Counting therefore recovers common ground and co-specialization recovers specialist corridors. We estimate the second, and accept the cost that the relational view is least informative precisely where every Party participates---which is why the descriptive claims below concern the structure of specialization, not the salience of an issue.

Let $x(a,i)$ denote the number of submissions by actor $a$ on topic $i$, where actors include consultative parties, non-consultative parties, observers, relevant organizations, and the ATS Secretariat whenever they appear as submitters in the archive. We define the normalized activity share
\begin{equation}\label{eq:activity_share}
\pi(a,i) = \frac{x(a,i)}{\sum_j x(a,j)},
\end{equation}

and the system-wide importance of topic $i$ as

\begin{equation}\label{eq:topic_importance}
Z_i = \frac{\sum_c x(c,i)}{\sum_{d,j} x(d,j)}.
\end{equation}

The Revealed Policy Advantage (RPA; a revealed comparative advantage (RCA)-style specialization index) is then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rpa}
    \rho(a,i) = \frac{\pi(a,i)}{Z_i}.
\end{equation}
We use the label \emph{revealed policy advantage} rather than the standard
\emph{revealed comparative advantage} to mark the shift from trade to governance.
The mathematics is unchanged because this is the same relative-specialization ratio
used in revealed-comparative-advantage work, relabeled for actors and policy topics rather than
countries and products.

Topics are linked by proximity
\begin{equation}\label{eq:proximity}
\phi(i,j)
=
\min\!\left\{
\begin{aligned}
    &\Pr\!\bigl(\rho(a,i) > 1 \mid \rho(a,j) > 1\bigr),\\
    &\Pr\!\bigl(\rho(a,j) > 1 \mid \rho(a,i) > 1\bigr)
\end{aligned}
\right\}.
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
        \Pr(\rho(a,j)>1 \mid \rho(a,i)>1) =\frac{\sum_a \mathbf{1}[\rho(a,i)>1 \land \rho(a,j)>1]}
        {\sum_a \mathbf{1}[\rho(a,i)>1]},
\end{equation}
yielding a weighted topic network. Taking the minimum of the two conditional probabilities serves three purposes. First, using conditional rather than joint probability controls for differences in topic prevalence, allowing two relatively rare topics to register as close if specialization in one reliably coincides with specialization in the other, whereas a joint probability would mechanically shrink with rarity. Second, taking the minimum prevents one-way dependence from overstating proximity. If a niche topic almost always appears alongside a broad topic, ($P(\text{broad}\mid \text{niche})$) may be high even when the reverse is low; the minimum forces the proximity to be limited by that weaker direction. Third, this yields a symmetric measure of relatedness, so the link between topics reflects mutual co-specialization rather than directional inclusion. Probabilities are estimated empirically across members within the relevant analysis window.
We construct both an aggregate space (for the mode-wide map) and time-resolved spaces by rebuilding proximities within each time window, allowing the topology itself to evolve rather than assuming a fixed structure over time; \cref{fig:space-ribbon-full} visualizes the actor-level support behind these time-resolved proximities as it shifts across windows.
This adapts the product-space proximity logic from economic complexity to governance concerns \cite{Hidalgo2007}.

\subsection{Constructing Modes from the Concern Space}
The three engagement modes in the main text are not obtained by clustering actors directly. Instead, they are defined as contiguous regions of the recovered topic space and actors are then assigned by how much of their excess portfolio mass falls into each region. This choice is deliberate because the substantive object of interest is movement and positioning \emph{within} a shared space of concerns, so the modes should respect that geometry rather than grouping actors by similarity alone.

We begin from the full actor-topic specialization matrix, not from an already aggregated topic profile. The aggregate proximity matrix $\phi$ in \cref{eq:proximity} is first constructed from repeated co-specialization across actors, so actor-level differences enter the mode procedure initially through the geometry of the space of concerns itself. We then convert $\phi$ into a weighted distance graph by assigning each positive edge the length
\begin{equation}
    d(i,j) = -\log\!\bigl(\max\{\phi(i,j), \varepsilon\}\bigr),
\end{equation}
with a small $\varepsilon>0$ to avoid taking logs of zero. We then compute all-pairs shortest-path distances on this topic graph and obtain a one-dimensional coordinate for each topic by classical multidimensional scaling (MDS). The resulting axis is used only as an ordered embedding that provides a consistent left-to-right representation of the space of concerns, not as a literal Euclidean policy spectrum.

As a projection diagnostic, the leading classical-MDS axis accounts for 23.8\% of the positive eigenvalue mass of the double-centered distance matrix; the first three axes account for 48.0\%, and the first five for 63.6\%. The largest spectral gap is between the first and second axes ($\lambda_1/\lambda_2=1.85$), indicating a dominant ordering gradient, but there is no sharp eigengap corresponding to three discrete clusters. We therefore use the 1D coordinate as a simplifying ordering of the full topic topology, not as a complete representation of the space of concerns. This is why the proximity, hazard, and generative-model analyses use the full pairwise space, while the modes serve as an interpretable contiguous summary of actor occupation along the dominant gradient.

To identify broad regions on this ordered space, we aggregate excess specialization across actors using
\begin{equation}
    g_i = \sum_a \max\{\rho(a,i)-1,0\},
\end{equation}
where $g_i$ is large when many actors place disproportionate attention on topic $i$. This aggregate signal is introduced only \emph{after} the space-of-concerns axis has been constructed. The mode fit itself is then performed on topic positions in the 1D embedding, not on actor observations directly. In the weighted specification used in the paper, we smooth $g_i$ over the 1D ordering with a short Gaussian kernel and locate $R=3$ region centers by an iterative 1D assignment-and-update step. Starting from three evenly spaced initial centers, each topic is assigned to its nearest center in the 1D embedding and each center is updated to the \emph{weighted} mean position of the topics currently assigned to it, using the smoothed excess-engagement signal as the weight. After convergence, each center is snapped to the nearest observed topic, and the mode boundaries are taken as the midpoints between adjacent centers. An unweighted comparison therefore corresponds to the same 1D procedure with equal topic weights. The geometry of the space of concerns remains actor-derived, but the partition itself is no longer pulled toward high-engagement parts of that space.

This procedure yields three contiguous topic blocks on the ordered space of concerns, so the modes summarize broad zones of issue concentration rather than unrestricted network communities. They are intentionally more structured than off-the-shelf actor clustering methods such as $k$-means, hierarchical clustering, or Leiden community detection, which can be useful descriptively but do not enforce the spatial contiguity needed for our interpretation of local movement and mode transitions. The space of concerns itself, a topology of joint interest, must therefore be distinguished from the weighted mode partition, which summarizes how actors occupy that topology. We selected $R=3$ as the minimum adequate partition rather than as a universal optimum. \cref{fig:regime-k-validation} reports two complementary checks using the same snapped weighted-center rule as the main analysis. First, weighted within-region inertia shows a clear elbow at $k=3$. Second, compressing actor portfolios to $k$-mode shares yields a large gain in preservation of pairwise actor similarity from $k=2$ to $k=3$, while additional modes provide only modest improvement and rapidly create very small residual regions. We therefore treat three modes as the smallest partition that captures the main portfolio geometry without over-fragmenting the space of concerns.

Actors are then positioned relative to this fixed topic partition. For actor $a$, let $r(i)\in\{1,2,3\}$ denote the mode assigned to topic $i$. We compute mode weights from the same excess specialization signal,
\begin{equation}
    \omega_{ar}
    =
    \frac{\sum_{i:r(i)=r} \max\{\rho(a,i)-1,0\}}
         {\sum_i \max\{\rho(a,i)-1,0\}},
\end{equation}
so that $\omega_{ar}$ is the share of actor $a$'s excess portfolio mass falling in mode $r$. The actor's dominant mode is the maximizer of $\omega_{ar}$, and the dominant-mode share $\max_r \omega_{ar}$ measures mode anchoring. This is the quantity used in the main-text discussion of strong aligners, mixed portfolios, and mode-persistence transitions. The complete topic-to-mode mapping is reported in \cref{tab:regime_topics_full}.

For descriptive mode-position summaries (\cref{fig:portfolio_space_ridgelines,tab:regime_alignment_full,fig:regime-engagement-panels,fig:fuzzy-regime-classes,fig:regime-tenure}), we retain actors with at least three specialized topics in the aggregate archive. For mode-transition matrices, we apply the analogous per-window requirement of at least three specialized topics before assigning a dominant mode. These filters avoid forcing centroid- and spread-based mode summaries onto one- or two-topic portfolios.


\subsection{Hazard Model for Local Topic Adoption}
To test whether new specialization is locally concentrated in the space of concerns, we estimate a rolling actor-level hazard model on adjacent year windows. For each actor $a$, topic $i$, and transition $t-1 \rightarrow t$, an observation is included only if the topic is at risk:
\[
\text{at-risk}_{a i t}=1 \iff \rho_{a i, t-1} < 1.
\]
The binary outcome is then
\[
y_{a i t}=1 \iff \rho_{a i, t} \ge 1 \ \text{and}\ \rho_{a i, t-1} < 1.
\]
Let the previously active support be
\[
S_{a,t-1}=\{j:\rho_{aj,t-1}\ge 1\},
\]
let the at-risk set be
\[
R_{at}=\{i:\rho_{ai,t-1}<1\},
\]
and let $m_{at}=\sum_{i\in R_{at}} y_{ait}$ denote the number of newly adopted topics in an actor-period. Our main locality specification is then a 5-year actor-period conditional logit,
\begin{dmath}
\Pr(\mathbf{y}_{at}\mid m_{at}, X_{at})
\propto
\exp\!\left(
\sum_{i\in R_{at}}
y_{ait}\bigl(
\beta_d d_{a i, t-1}
\!+\!
\beta_p \mathrm{pop}_{i,t-1}
\bigr)
\right),
\end{dmath}
where
\[
d_{a i, t-1}=1-\max_{j\in S_{a,t-1}}\phi(i,j)
\]
is the raw distance from candidate topic $i$ to the actor's prior portfolio and $\mathrm{pop}_{i,t-1}$ is prior topic popularity (share of actors with $\rho \ge 1$). The model conditions on the total number of adoption events $m_{at}$ within each actor-period and on an actor-period-specific intercept, so identification comes from which at-risk topics are selected inside a given choice set rather than from cross-panel differences in baseline adoption rates. Actor-periods with no prior support or no new adoptions therefore do not contribute to the likelihood, and prior breadth is absorbed into the conditioned actor-period effect rather than estimated as a separate slope.
In the main specification, this leaves 1,055 actor-period choice sets and 40,129 at-risk topic observations, with $\beta_{\mathrm{distance}}=-2.93$ (SE $0.18$; 95\% CI $[-3.27, -2.59]$). Because the rolling windows overlap, successive choice sets for the same actor are not statistically independent and the reported intervals do not adjust for this dependence; the distance effect exceeds sixteen times its standard error and is stable across all three space constructions (\cref{fig:hazard-space-sensitivity}). Distances are computed from a cumulative-lagged space of concerns built using only information available up to $t-1$ and kept on the raw $1-\phi$ scale. This choice is intentional because the cumulative-lagged space provides the strictest prior-information version of the locality test while raw $1-\phi$ remains bounded, directly interpretable, and aligned with the positive $\phi$-fit term used in the generative retain-and-adopt model below. \cref{fig:hazard-space-sensitivity} compares the same conditional-logit specification under aggregate full-history and instantaneous previous-window spaces. A log transformation $-\log \phi$ is more natural for longer-range connectivity, but in these data the mass of zero-proximity ties creates a long right tail in the non-aggregate spaces, so the primary hazard analysis stays on raw $1-\phi$.

\subsection{Persistence and Mode-Transition Diagnostics}
To summarize persistence after adoption, we construct actor-topic specialization spells on 1-year windows. A spell begins in the first year for which $\rho_{ait} \ge 1$ and is then followed forward year by year. In the retention panel of \cref{fig:retention-curves}, a spell is considered ended only after inactivity persists beyond an allowed gap length. We therefore report Kaplan--Meier curves under one- to five-gap tolerance rules, where a 1-gap tolerance treats a spell as ended only after two consecutive years with $\rho_{ait} < 1$, and larger tolerances progressively smooth over short interruptions. Spells that remain active through the end of the archive are right-censored in 2025. This panel is descriptive rather than a separate regression model; its role is to show that once specialized attention is acquired it tends to persist.

For mode-persistence diagnostics, we assign each actor-window a dominant mode from region shares computed on $\max(\rho-1,0)$ over the fixed three-region topic partition used in \cref{fig:portfolio_space_ridgelines}, exclude actor-windows with fewer than three specialized topics, and then tabulate actor transitions across adjacent windows. Because this partition is derived from the pooled archive, the transition diagnostics describe the stability of occupation on a fixed long-run map rather than a strictly out-of-sample prediction; the adoption test above, built on the cumulative-lagged space, provides the prior-information counterpart. The main text reports rolling 5-year windows, while \cref{fig:regime-window-sensitivity} compares these with the more tactical 1-year specification. We summarize the resulting transition process with same-mode, adjacent-or-same, and far-jump ($1\leftrightarrow3$) rates, and display the corresponding row-normalized transition matrix in \cref{fig:hazard-distance-timeseries}A.

\subsection{Sequential Retain-and-Adopt Model of Portfolio Dynamics}

To test whether the Treaty-centered record can be approximated by a simple generative mechanism, we fit a reduced-form model conditioned on three observed objects: (i) the active actor set in each year, (ii) actor-specific budgets $K_{at}$, and (iii) a fixed pooled space-of-concerns matrix $\phi$ from \cref{eq:proximity}. The goal is not to explain who becomes active or how budgets arise, but whether the observed organization of interests can be reproduced once those constraints are given.

A single-rule support equation proved too blunt because it conflated topic retention and topic entry. We therefore model portfolio change sequentially. Let $x_{ait}$ denote actor $a$'s submission mass on topic $i$ at time $t$, let $x_{at}$ denote the corresponding topic-allocation vector, and let $K_{at}=\sum_i x_{ait}$ be the observed total budget in that year. Define the support indicator $z_{ait}=1[x_{ait}>0]$ and the support set $S_{at}=\{i:z_{ait}=1\}$. We write $s_{ai,t-1}=x_{ai,t-1}/K_{a,t-1}$ for the prior topic share and $p_{i,t-1}$ for prior topic popularity. The latent Bernoulli draws $z^{\mathrm{ret}}_{ait}$ and $z^{\mathrm{ent}}_{ait}$ indicate whether a previously held topic is retained and whether a previously absent topic is entered, respectively; $\alpha_i^{\mathrm{ret}}$, $\alpha_i^{\mathrm{ent}}$, and $\alpha_i$ are topic-specific intercepts, while $\delta_{\mathrm{ret}}$ and $\delta_{\mathrm{ent}}$ are stage-specific intercept shifts. For topics already in the portfolio, retention is
\begin{dmath}
\Pr\!\left(z^{\mathrm{ret}}_{ait}=1 \mid z_{ai,t-1}=1\right)
=
\mathrm{logit}^{-1}\!\Bigl(
\alpha_i^{\mathrm{ret}}
\!+\!
\delta_{\mathrm{ret}}
\!+\!
\lambda_{\mathrm{ret}} s_{ai,t-1}
\!+\!
\gamma_{\mathrm{ret}} p_{i,t-1}
\Bigr).
\end{dmath}
For topics not yet held, entry is
\begin{dmath}
\Pr\!\left(z^{\mathrm{ent}}_{ait}=1 \mid z_{ai,t-1}=0\right)
=
\mathrm{logit}^{-1}\!\Bigl(
\alpha_i^{\mathrm{ent}}
\!+\!
\delta_{\mathrm{ent}}
\!+\!
\beta_{\mathrm{ent}}\,\mathrm{Fit}_{ait}
\!+\!
\gamma_{\mathrm{ent}} p_{i,t-1}
\Bigr),
\end{dmath}
with
\begin{dmath}
\mathrm{Fit}_{ait}
=
\frac{1}{|S_{a,t-1}|}
\sum_{j\in S_{a,t-1}}
\phi(i,j),
\end{dmath}
so new topics are favored when they are close to the actor's prior support in the native space of concerns. The same $\mathrm{Fit}_{ait}$ term enters the allocation stage below as the average $\phi$-proximity of candidate topic $i$ to the actor's previous support.

After combining retained and entered topics into the support set $S_{at}$, the observed budget $K_{at}$ is allocated across the selected support by
\begin{dmath}
x_{at} \sim \mathrm{Multinomial}\!\Bigl(K_{at}, \mathrm{softmax}\!\Bigl( \alpha_i \!+\! \rho s_{ai,t-1} \!+\! \beta\,\mathrm{Fit}_{ait} \!+\! \gamma p_{i,t-1}\Bigr)_{i\in S_{at}} \Bigr).
\end{dmath}
Topic intercepts $\alpha_i$, $\alpha_i^{\mathrm{ent}}$, and $\alpha_i^{\mathrm{ret}}$ are empirical baselines estimated from pooled topic mass, pooled entry frequency, and pooled retention frequency. The free coefficients $(\rho,\beta,\gamma)$, $(\delta_{\mathrm{ent}},\beta_{\mathrm{ent}},\gamma_{\mathrm{ent}})$, and $(\delta_{\mathrm{ret}},\lambda_{\mathrm{ret}},\gamma_{\mathrm{ret}})$ are estimated by maximum likelihood. A final scalar intercept calibration is then applied to the entry and retention logits during simulation to align support levels while keeping the fitted slopes fixed.

Despite its simplicity, the sequential retain-and-adopt model performs well because the fitted allocation stage remains strongly persistent and local ($\rho=4.29$, $\beta=3.97$), the entry stage is also local ($\beta_{\mathrm{ent}}=0.58$), and the retention-stage prior-share effect remains positive in point estimate ($\lambda_{\mathrm{ret}}=0.42$). After the intercept calibration, the model reproduces mean actor breadth closely (observed $3.35$, simulated $3.32$), mean topic popularity almost exactly (observed $2.45$, simulated $2.45$), temporal variation in breadth ($r=0.87$), temporal variation in topic popularity ($r=0.98$), and local entry rank in $\phi$-space (observed $0.677$, simulated $0.675$). The main implication is that a large share of Treaty-centered portfolio structure can be generated by selective persistence, local entry, and constrained allocation, without requiring actors to diffuse uniformly across the issue space.

Approximate parameter intervals support the same interpretation, with allocation persistence and local fit remaining clearly positive ($\rho=4.29$, 95\% Wald interval $[3.87,4.71]$; $\beta=3.97$, $[2.92,5.02]$) and the entry stage remaining positive and local ($\beta_{\mathrm{ent}}=0.58$, $[0.19,0.97]$). The retention-stage prior-share effect is positive and more clearly identified under the year-based fit ($\lambda_{\mathrm{ret}}=0.42$, $[0.11,0.74]$; \cref{fig:split-support-params}). By contrast, the retention-stage popularity term remains imprecise and overlaps zero, suggesting that the robust part of the mechanism lies in persistence and local entry rather than in a strong retention response to topic-wide popularity.

\subsection{Pioneering Timing and Mode Position}
To characterize how early agenda entry relates to the mode structure, we define an actor-level pioneer index and then relate it to breadth and mode position. This index is intended as a descriptive proxy for earlier entry into topics that later become mainstream in the Treaty discussion record, not as a direct measure of control over final decisions.

First, we establish a topic emergence year for each policy topic. This is defined as the first year in which the cumulative number of submissions on that topic reaches 15\% of its total historical volume. This metric identifies when a topic transitions from a niche issue to part of the mainstream agenda.

Second, we determine an actor entry year for each actor-topic pair. Using a 5-year rolling window to smooth out short-term fluctuations, we calculate RPA for every actor in every topic for each window. An actor is considered to have ``entered'' a topic in the first year when its RPA value is greater than or equal to 1.0, signifying specialization.

Third, we calculate a relative pioneer index. For each actor-topic specialization, we define the ``relative lag'' as the difference between the actor's entry year and the topic's emergence year (or the actor's first year of activity, whichever is later). A negative lag indicates pioneering behavior, i.e. adopting a topic before it becomes mainstream, while a positive lag indicates a follower. The pioneer index for a given actor is the negative of its average relative lag across all topics it specializes in, such that a higher, positive score corresponds to earlier adoption; the index is measured in years.

Finally, we summarize each actor's mode position using the same three-region partition employed in \cref{fig:portfolio_space_ridgelines}. Let $\omega_{ar}$ denote actor $a$'s share of excess specialization signal in mode $r$, and let $m_a=\max_r \omega_{ar}$ denote the actor's mode anchoring. We then estimate a simple cross-sectional model
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{PioneerIndex}_a = \alpha + \beta\,\mathrm{Breadth}_a + \lambda\,m_a + \delta_{R(a)} + \varepsilon_a,
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{Breadth}_a$ is the number of specialized topics and $R(a)$ is the actor's dominant mode. In the fitted data, both breadth ($\beta=0.26$, $p=0.0014$) and mode anchoring ($\lambda=17.70$, $p=1.5\times10^{-4}$) are positively associated with earlier entry, while dominant-mode indicators are not distinguishable from zero. This is why the main-text pioneer result is framed as an exploratory earlier-entry association tied to mode position and anchoring rather than as a distinct ``pioneer type'' or a direct measure of agenda-setting power.

Because breadth is mechanically related to opportunity, we also estimate auxiliary OLS specifications that add logged topic assignments, archive tenure, consultative/non-consultative status, and original-signatory status. These checks are not used for causal identification; they bound the interpretation of the pioneer result by asking whether the anchoring association is only a proxy for actor size, time in the archive, or Treaty status. Across these auxiliary specifications, mode anchoring remains positive (coefficients 14.1--18.0, all $p<0.01$), while breadth remains positive but is more sensitive to the opportunity and formal-status controls. None of these specifications adjusts for external capability, such as the scale or budget of national Antarctic programs, which plausibly shapes both breadth and entry timing; this is a further reason to read the pioneer analysis as descriptive rather than causal.

\subsection{Pairwise Complementarity under Strong Mode Anchoring}
The complementarity analysis in Supplementary Results asks whether strongly anchored sovereign-state actors cover distinct parts of the agenda or mostly duplicate one another. We therefore restrict attention to sovereign states with at least five specialized topics in the aggregate archive and dominant-mode share at least $0.70$. For the hard-mode comparison, each actor is labeled by its dominant mode. For the fuzzy comparison, an actor is assigned to every mode whose share is at least $0.25$, defaulting back to the dominant mode if no mode exceeds that threshold.

Let $S_i$ denote actor $i$'s set of specialized topics ($\rho>1$) in the aggregate archive. For each pair, we summarize complementary coverage by the distinct-topic share
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\left|\bigcup_i S_i\right|}{\sum_i |S_i|},
\end{equation}
and redundancy by the pairwise Jaccard overlap on the specialized-topic sets. Because both quantities depend mechanically on support size, we residualize them against a size-matched null. For each observed pair, we subtract the mean over nearby pairs in standardized support-size space, using the ordered pair of specialized-topic counts $(k_{\mathrm{small}}, k_{\mathrm{large}})$ as the matching coordinates. We then average these residuals by mode-pair or fuzzy-pair class. Significance is assessed by permutation tests that shuffle dominant-mode or fuzzy-class labels across the anchored actor set while preserving the observed class counts.

The institutional profile analysis in \cref{fig:regime-pair-institutional} uses the anchored hard-mode pairs and compares Mode 1--3 pairs against all other anchored mode pairs. We summarize the frequency of mixed Consultative Party/Non-Consultative Party status, gateway-state combinations, claimant involvement, reserving non-claimant involvement, and same-community co-sponsorship membership, and evaluate those contrasts with permutation tests over the pair labels. These pair-level diagnostics are intended as an interpretive supplement to the main complementarity result rather than as an independent partition of the space of concerns.

This subsection reports robustness checks tied directly to the main empirical claims: pioneer timing, local adoption, and mode persistence. A separate subsection below addresses robustness of the space-of-concerns construction itself.

Because early-adoption metrics depend on topic-emergence timing, we also recomputed topic emergence years over cumulative-volume thresholds from 5\% to 30\% (\cref{fig:emergence-threshold-sensitivity}). Median emergence timing remains stable, and shifts relative to the 15\% baseline are generally small across topics, indicating that the pioneer timing results are not driven by a single percentile choice.

We also tested whether locality findings depend on how the space of concerns is constructed. \Cref{fig:hazard-space-sensitivity} compares the same 5-year conditional-logit hazard under three definitions of the space of concerns: aggregate full-history, instantaneous previous-window, and cumulative-lagged (all data up to $t-1$), always using raw distance $d=1-\max\phi$ to the prior portfolio. The main text uses the cumulative-lagged specification because it relies only on prior information, while the aggregate and instantaneous spaces serve as lower-variance and higher-volatility benchmarks, respectively. We keep raw $1-\phi$ as the primary scale because it is bounded and aligns directly with the positive $\phi$-fit term in the retain-and-adopt model. A log transformation is conceptually attractive for longer-range connectivity, but here it is less stable because zero-proximity ties generate a long right tail outside the aggregate space.

Two further checks address mechanical concerns about the proximity construction itself. First, each actor's own submission history contributes to the proximities used to compute its distances, which could in principle build a self-fulfilling element into the locality test. We therefore recomputed all distances leave-one-actor-out: for every actor, the space of concerns is re-estimated with that actor's submissions removed before RPA and proximity estimation, holding the outcome panel fixed. The actor-excluded spaces are nearly identical to the full space (mean off-diagonal correlation $0.99$), and the locality effect attenuates only modestly while remaining strong (cumulative-lagged: $\beta_{\mathrm{distance}}=-2.57$, 95\% CI $[-2.90, -2.24]$, odds ratio per $0.1$ distance $0.77$; aggregate: $\beta=-3.36$). Second, full counting credits a co-sponsored paper once to every sponsor, which mechanically generates co-specialization among frequent co-sponsors. Fractional attribution is the established remedy in this archive: \textcite{DudeneyWalton2012} adopt it as their primary counting scheme for Antarctic working papers, with additional weight given to the first-named Party and separate treatment of the two meeting venues. We use the simpler even split, since the first-named convention is not consistently applied across the full 1961--2025 span, and treat the result as a robustness check rather than as the primary construction. Rebuilding the space with fractional counting, in which each paper contributes $1/n$ to each of its $n$ sponsors, changes the aggregate proximity matrix appreciably (off-diagonal correlation with the full-count space $r=0.84$) but leaves the locality estimate essentially unchanged (cumulative-lagged: $\beta_{\mathrm{distance}}=-2.84$, 95\% CI $[-3.18, -2.50]$; aggregate: $\beta=-3.53$). Local adoption in the space of concerns is therefore not an artifact of actors' self-contribution to the proximity estimates, nor of co-sponsorship inflation.

A third check addresses the interpretation of the space rather than its stability. No edge is computed from text, so the space is not a semantic construction by definition; but because the topic labels are the Secretariat's own natural-language phrases, the recovered geometry could still happen to track how those phrases are worded. We test that coincidence directly, comparing the proximity matrix against a TF-IDF cosine similarity computed over the same 45 labels, under both word (1--2 gram, English stop words removed) and character (3--5 gram, word-boundary) analyzers with sublinear term-frequency scaling. Significance is assessed by a Mantel permutation with $10{,}000$ joint row-and-column shuffles, which preserves the dependence among the $990$ pairwise entries that invalidates an ordinary correlation test. Lexical similarity is positively but weakly associated with proximity, explaining $1.2\%$ of its variance for word $n$-grams ($r=0.111$, Spearman $\rho=0.079$, $p=0.002$) and $1.8\%$ for character $n$-grams ($r=0.134$, $\rho=0.153$, $p=0.001$). The residual association is what shared institutional vocabulary would produce---many labels contain ``Antarctic'' or ``environmental''---and it leaves the geometry essentially unexplained by language. A lexical rather than neural baseline is deliberate, so that the comparison requires no external model and reproduces exactly; it also tests the objection in its strongest practical form, since the labels are short administrative phrases whose overlap is mostly literal. \texttt{scripts/semantic\_baseline.py} reproduces these numbers and writes the full pair table.

To verify that mode-persistence claims are not an artifact of a specific temporal aggregation, we compare dominant-mode transitions under 1-year and rolling 5-year windows in \cref{fig:regime-window-sensitivity}. The 1-year window captures more tactical switching, while the 5-year window captures the longer-run center of gravity; in both cases, transitions remain overwhelmingly local (same or adjacent mode), with rare direct Mode 1$\leftrightarrow$Mode 3 jumps.

\subsection{Robustness of the Concern Space}
The space of concerns itself can be stress-tested by removing actors prior to construction and asking how much the resulting topic-topic proximity matrix changes. We proceed greedily by removing, at each step, the actor whose exclusion produces the largest root mean squared difference from the current full-space proximity matrix, then repeating the procedure. This does not test a substantive behavioral claim directly; it asks whether the shared topology of concerns is jointly produced or dominated by a small number of actors.

In \cref{fig:sensitivity-analysis}, the space of concerns changes gradually through much of the removal sequence, indicating that the topology is not driven by any single actor. The earliest removals are concentrated among long-established ATS actors and central observer organizations, but the curve steepens only late in the process, when the remaining actor set becomes too thin to sustain the original structure. We therefore interpret the space of concerns as robustly co-produced because, although some actors matter more than others, distributed contributions rather than a lone structural anchor sustain the geometry.

That robustness result should be read together with the temporal mosaic in \cref{fig:decadal-space-stack}. The pooled space of concerns used in the main text is analytically useful, but it remains a long-run composition of historically layered configurations rather than a claim that the latent geometry is literally fixed from decade to decade.

    \label{fig:who-enters-first}
\end{figure*}

Actors that enter emerging issues earlier are not concentrated in any one mode; they are distinguished instead by broad but anchored portfolios. Across the 64 actors with a defined pioneer index, early entrants appear in all three modes rather than clustering in one privileged bloc (\cref{fig:pioneer_plot}). Throughout, ``agenda'' is used in a narrow document-system sense---which concerns are raised early enough, and often enough, to become part of the Treaty-centered discussion record---not control over final consensus outcomes.

What distinguishes earlier movers is not mode membership alone, but position within the space. Actors that enter issues earlier tend to be both broader and more strongly anchored within one mode rather than evenly mixed across the full space. A simple ordinary least squares (OLS) regression using the relative pioneer index (measured in years of average entry lead) as the outcome ($R^2=0.24$, $n=64$) shows that both breadth ($\beta_{\mathrm{breadth}}=0.26$ years per specialized topic, $p=0.0014$) and max mode share ($\beta_{\mathrm{anchor}}=17.70$, roughly $1.8$ years of additional lead per $0.1$ increase in dominant-mode share, $p=1.5\times10^{-4}$) are positively associated with earlier entry, while dominant-mode indicators are not distinguishable from zero (\cref{fig:pioneer_plot_residual}). The breadth term should be read partly as opportunity: actors with broader portfolios have more chances to appear early on at least some topics, and the specification does not adjust for external capabilities such as the scale of national Antarctic programs.

This pattern is stable to alternative cumulative-volume thresholds for defining topic emergence (\cref{fig:emergence-threshold-sensitivity}). Auxiliary specifications that add logged topic assignments, archive tenure, consultative/non-consultative status, and original-signatory status leave mode anchoring positive (coefficients 14.1--18.0, all $p<0.01$). The breadth association is more model-sensitive: because broad, strongly anchored, and long-tenured actors are largely the same small set of long-established participants, breadth, anchoring, and original-signatory status cannot be cleanly separated at this sample size ($n=64$), so we identify structured position as associated with earlier entry without isolating which of these channels drives it. We therefore interpret this result as an exploratory cross-sectional association between earlier agenda entry, in this restricted discussion-setting sense, and structured position in the space of concerns, rather than as a causal identification of control over final outcomes. The result is not that one bloc controls the Treaty agenda. It is that earlier entry is associated with actors that combine routes through the agenda with a clearer institutional anchor, giving them more ways to attach emerging concerns to established pathways.

This pair-level association (\cref{fig:portfolio-share-collaboration}) is exactly what we would expect if co-sponsorship partly tracks shared concern positions. At the same time, it does not collapse the space-of-concerns result into a simple collaboration network because even among collaborating pairs, overlap varies substantially, and repeated co-submission is far from a deterministic proxy for space-of-concerns similarity. Co-sponsorship therefore provides a useful adjacent view of ATS coordination, but it does not replace the issue-space perspective developed in the main text.

Simple external correlates do less explanatory work. Geographic distance shows no clear negative overlap gradient, European Union (EU) and North Atlantic Treaty Organization (NATO) pairings are not unusually aligned under permutation, and only Brazil-Russia-India-China-South Africa (BRICS) pairs show a positive signal---but on a much smaller ATS sample. Because the bloc indicators use current official memberships rather than time-varying historical memberships, these checks should be read as a coarse grouping check rather than a period-specific geopolitical test. These checks therefore point back toward ATS-native positioning rather than broad geography or bloc politics as the more informative lens (\cref{fig:geo-portfolio-overlap,fig:bloc-overlap}).
