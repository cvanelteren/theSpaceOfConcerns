\begin{abstract}
Consensus-based regimes govern critical global commons, yet unanimity obscures how agendas evolve before formal policies are enacted. Here, we analyse 64 years of archival records from the Antarctic Treaty Consultative Meetings (1961--2025), mapping them into a quantitative "space of concerns", an empirical network of issue proximity adapted from economic complexity. We find that agenda expansion is strongly path-dependent: actors enter new policy concerns primarily near those they already sustain. Engagement profiles are similarly persistent, while actors with broader, better-anchored portfolios tend to enter emerging concerns earlier, although this association is descriptive rather than causal. As pressures from tourism, genetic resources, and environmental change intensify, these findings show how institutional histories shape the capacity to sustain emerging concerns. Mapping these pathways provides a diagnostic for identifying barriers to agenda participation and testing reforms in shared environmental governance.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} agenda formation, consensus governance, global commons, environmental governance, space of concerns, network analysis, Antarctic Treaty

\noindent\textbf{Science for Society}
\newline
Environmental problems that did not exist when an institution was built, such as climate change or new biological technologies, still have to compete for a seat on that institution's agenda. In consensus regimes like the Antarctic Treaty System, which decide only when everyone agrees, that competition leaves no formal record: there are no votes to count, so the usual way of tracking who pushed for what is unavailable. We instead tracked it through 64 years and 6,573 working papers submitted to Treaty meetings, mapping which issues the same members repeatedly work on together into a single map of the agenda. Members overwhelmingly move into issues near the ones they already work on, and how far they can reach beyond that neighborhood depends less on how prominent an issue is than on how many members already work on issues near it. That pattern falls hardest on the issues that arrived after the Treaty was built: climate change and biological prospecting sit on some of the most isolated ground in the system, and the members who joined between 1991 and 2010 started with markedly less room to move than earlier members did. This points to a concrete, testable lever, which is helping members connect an emerging issue to one they already work on, rather than the vaguer goal of building capacity in general.

\section{Introduction}
International governance is usually read through its outcomes: the treaties signed, the protocols ratified, the measures adopted \cite{Keohane1984,Young2011Effectiveness}. An outcome is an endpoint. Years before an instrument exists, an institution has already made quieter choices about which problems deserve sustained attention and which do not \cite{BachrachBaratz1962,Kingdon1984}, and those choices constrain what any final agreement can be \cite{Drubel2023HiddenContestation}. Consensus regimes hide this earlier stage. Unanimity produces no roll calls, so there is no tally of who stood where, and the positioning that matters most, through agenda-setting, issue linkage, and repeated attention to selected concerns \cite{Steinberg2002,Stone2013,Vabulas2013}, leaves no formal record.

Consensus institutions do keep a meticulous documentary record. Working papers and information documents show which concerns actors raise, sustain, and connect, and that record is the observable trace of agenda formation. We read sixty-four years of it as a single object: 6,573 working papers and information documents submitted to the Antarctic Treaty Consultative Meetings between 1961 and 2025, from 66 actors across 45 topics, classified under the Secretariat's own labels (Methods; Supplementary Information \cref{fig:topic-submission-overview}). The Antarctic Treaty System is the longest-running regime governing a planetary commons. The 1959 Treaty demilitarized the continent and reserved it for peaceful purposes, and its meetings decide by consensus as settled practice \cite{AntarcticTreaty1959,Joyner1998,Yao2021,Kennicutt2019,Sampaio2022}. It is also a demanding test of what documents can show, since some of today's most pressing concerns, climate change and biological prospecting among them, are ones the founding instruments never named.

We build the \emph{space of concerns}, a network whose nodes are the concerns themselves. An actor holds a \emph{Revealed Policy Advantage} in a topic when it devotes a disproportionate share of its submissions to it; two topics sit close when the same actors hold advantages in both. Actors therefore occupy neighborhoods rather than lists, and because a move to an adjacent concern is easier than a move to a distant one, where an actor can go next is set by where it already is. The existing quantitative literature on agendas counts attention: how much an issue receives and who supplies it \cite{BaumgartnerJones1993,JonesBaumgartner2005,GreenPedersenWalgrave2014,DudeneyWalton2012,Sanchez2016}. A count cannot say which concerns are held together, how one actor's concerns sit relative to another's, or which concern an actor could plausibly take up next. We measure that missing relation directly. The closest precedent is economic complexity, which infers the relatedness of products from co-specialization and documents local diversification across products, industries, regions, and research fields \cite{Hidalgo2007,Hidalgo2018,Neffke2011,Boschma2017,Guevara2016}. Governance is a hard case for the transplant, with no exports, prices, or market selection to make specialization visible.

Three scope conditions bound the reading. The space can place only actors that submit; a party that never files a paper leaves no trace in the archive and holds no position, and the record is thin at the bottom, with 12 of the 66 actors filing fewer than five papers, five of them exactly one. That censoring runs in the same direction as the participation question this paper raises, because the actors least visible to the instrument are exactly the ones a widening-participation lever would target. The topics are the Secretariat's own harmonized labels, and the Secretariat both assigns those labels and submits papers under its own name, so the recovered structure partly reflects the institution's own taxonomy. We test the geometry against that reading (the lexical baseline and the instrument mapping in Supplementary Information) rather than assume it escapes. The unit of analysis is the submitting actor, for whom \emph{agenda capacity} means the ability to keep issues present, connect them to established procedure, and move into related concerns, while \emph{agenda} means the document-system agenda. Nothing that follows speaks to control over final consensus outcomes.

Four results follow, each feeding the next. The archive yields a legible map of the institution, and the map is not a restatement of either the topics' wording or the Treaty's architecture. Portfolios move along that map, not across it, and what blocks entry at range is the breadth of the portfolios that already hold a concern, a barrier that tightens with distance. Because the ground on offer shifts as the archive fills, equal access does not produce equal portfolios: members who joined between 1991 and 2010 built on thinner ground than earlier entrants, and the concerns the founding instruments never named, climate change and biological prospecting among them, sit on the least reachable ground of all. Finally, the map works as an instrument, predicting where portfolios expand next and carrying signals that counting misses. Because concerns are taken up through adjacent ones, the practical lever is adjacency: shorten the distance between an emerging concern and one a Party already sustains. That is a narrower, more testable target than general capacity-building (\cref{sec:lowering-barriers}).

\section{Results}
\subsection{Reading the Institution from Its Documents}

The space of concerns is a map of the agenda, and its shape is legible against the institution that produced it. A dense procedural and environmental core sits at the center: opening statements, exchange of information, environmental monitoring and reporting, environmental protection, liability, inspections, the day-to-day machinery through which the regime does its routine business. The core is procedural rather than substantive. Opening statements are formulaic annual submissions, filed by 29 of 66 actors against a median of 13 across concerns and held by unusually narrow portfolios (mean portfolio breadth $11.3$ against a median of $14.9$), so the center records routine engagement that many portfolios share. From that core, thinner branches run toward resource and science frontiers, tourism and visitation, and operational coordination (\cref{fig:the_space_of_concerns}a).

Two artifact readings have to be excluded before the map can be used, and wording is the first. Lexical similarity explains only $1$--$2\%$ of the variance in proximity (TF-IDF cosine, Mantel $p\le0.002$; Methods): Exchange of information and Opening statements share no vocabulary and are among the closest concerns ($\phi=0.52$), while the lexically similar Comprehensive Environmental Evaluations and State of the Antarctic Environment Report sit far apart ($\phi=0.07$). The second is the Treaty's own architecture. A concern's strongest tie stays inside its governing instrument only $32\%$ of the time, against a $17\%$ baseline (\cref{fig:space-by-instrument}). Two Treaty constraints are nonetheless legible in the geometry. The 1959 Treaty demilitarized the continent and reserved it for peaceful purposes \cite{AntarcticTreaty1959,Joyner1998,Yao2021}, and the 1991 Madrid Protocol prohibits mineral-resource activity other than scientific research \cite{MadridProtocol1991,Bastmeijer2003,Rothwell1996,Koivurova2005}. A resource-science corridor links management and assessment to environmental classification, marine acoustics, drilling, and sub-glacial lakes, a neighborhood where scientific value and strategic relevance can coexist; the topology says nothing about motives and does not make science conflict in disguise \cite{Brady2011,Brady2017,Sampaio2022,HansenMagnusson2022,Szpak2025}. Access pressures enter through conservation and safety obligations rather than distributive bargaining, with inspections as the compliance hinge \cite{Haward2012,Goldsworthy2022,Meyer2022,Luo2024AntarcticTourism}. Terms such as ``core'', ``corridor'', and ``chain'' describe visually dense or elongated parts of the network; they are not separately estimated clusters.

Beyond those checks, the map has to hold together as an object, and it does. The space is broadly connected but bounded: only 37 of 990 topic pairs sit at zero proximity ($3.74\%$), and no topic is fully isolated (Supplementary Information \cref{fig:heatmap_concerns}). It is jointly sustained rather than inherited from any one actor or period. Link density, giant-component size, and closeness rise as participation expands after the 1990s, greedy actor-removal produces only gradual change, and shortest paths mean two concerns with little direct co-specialization can still sit close through shared intermediaries (Supplementary Information \cref{fig:space_network_metrics,fig:sensitivity-analysis}). What does not hold up is any partition of the space. Against a null that preserves every actor's portfolio size and every concern's holder count, $90\%$ of concern pairs fall within two standard deviations of what degree alone predicts, and twelve partitioning schemes, from interval cuts to block models, agree with their own bootstrap replicates at adjusted Rand indices no better than $0.22$--$0.33$. The four concern classes used below reproduce at $0.30$, comfortably above a $0.00$ chance floor (95\% interval $[-0.05,+0.07]$) and no better than any other way of cutting the space (\texttt{scripts/space\_null\_and\_partitions.py}). The classes are a reading device, a way to talk about a well-connected space, and the paper never claims that concerns come in four kinds. A map that faithfully records the institution should also govern how actors move through it. Whether it does is the next test.

\subsection{How Agendas Grow}

It does. For each actor and each pair of adjacent rolling 5-year windows, we compare where its newly specialized topics sit relative to its prior portfolio against a size-matched redraw from the topics it did not already hold, so breadth, era, and activity volume cancel within the pair. Of the 1,055 expansion events in the record, which comprise 1,895 individual topic moves, $83.0\%$ land nearer than their own null (median shift $-0.068$ in $1-\max\phi$; Wilcoxon $p<10^{-15}$), falling to $66.5\%$ under the strictest construction and remaining decisively above chance there (Supplementary Information \cref{sec:paired-null-robustness}). Separately, $46.9\%$ of actor-transitions add no new specialization. Modeled rather than counted, over 1,055 actor-period choice sets and 40,129 at-risk topic observations, a $0.1$ increase in distance multiplies the odds that an at-risk topic is adopted, relative to the other candidates in that actor-period, by $0.75$ (95\% CI $[0.72,0.77]$; $\beta_{\mathrm{distance}}=-2.93$, $[-3.27,-2.59]$), net of a positive control for prior topic popularity. \Cref{fig:class-story}a shows the same gradient nonparametrically on the raw distance scale. Portfolios move slowly, and they move locally.

A result built from the archive could be an artifact of how the space itself is constructed, so we rebuilt it five ways; the odds ratios span $0.64$--$0.77$ and none approaches the no-effect line (Supplementary Information \cref{fig:hazard-distance-timeseries}d). The gradient is steeper in the pooled full-history space and persists in the more volatile previous-window space, so it does not hang on one choice of temporal aggregation (Supplementary Information \cref{fig:hazard-space-sensitivity}). Two further constructions are more demanding. The first recomputes each actor's distances from a space estimated without that actor's own submissions, leaving $\beta_{\mathrm{distance}}=-2.57$; the second weights co-sponsored papers fractionally across sponsors, leaving $-2.84$. The second is the harder test, because dropping one actor barely perturbs the geometry ($r=0.99$) whereas fractional counting rebuilds the proximity matrix appreciably ($r=0.84$). The estimate also survives the dependence that rolling windows induce and the composition of the risk set: an actor-level bootstrap returns the same interval, a non-overlapping-window panel gives OR $0.79$ $[0.76,0.82]$, a states-only panel $0.76$ $[0.73,0.78]$, and a topic-age control $0.74$ $[0.72,0.77]$ (Methods). Local adoption is a temporally predictive feature of the archive, not a by-product of reconstructing the topology from the full record.

Locality is also uneven across the terrain. Of the 1,871 class-assigned expansion moves, $45\%$ land on Gateway ground and only $6\%$ on Cul-de-sac (\cref{fig:the_space_of_concerns}c), a concentration that tracks how much attention each kind of ground already carries. Once that attention gradient is accounted for, one barrier governs entry, and it is holder breadth. Net of distance and of how much attention a concern already attracts, the breadth of the portfolios sustaining a concern predicts whether an actor takes it up (odds ratio $0.89$ per unit of holder breadth, 95\% CI $[0.87,0.92]$, $p=5\times10^{-15}$; \texttt{scripts/entry\_barrier\_regression.py}). Reach, by comparison, carries only a small positive association (odds ratio $1.05$ per additional reachable concern, $[1.04,1.06]$, $p=1\times10^{-19}$), and a pooled logistic on the full at-risk set of 79,706 observations reproduces both signs (holder breadth $0.87$; reach $1.05$). The holder penalty strengthens with distance, from $0.91$ at the tenth percentile of observed distance to $0.84$ at the ninetieth (interaction odds ratio $0.85$ per unit of raw $1-\max\phi$, $p=0.02$). Close in, uptake follows attention. Further out, prior attention stops being sufficient, and who currently sustains a concern becomes the binding constraint. These are popularity-adjusted odds ratios, not the unadjusted rate ratios of the binned descriptives below; nulls separating this from proximity and from prior popularity are reported in \cref{sec:class-validation}.

Classifying each of the 1,871 expansion moves by the class it stepped off and the class it landed on (\cref{fig:class-story}b; the class panels use the rolling-RCA construction, which recovers 1,871 of the 1,895 moves counted above; Supplementary Information Table~\ref{tab:locality_estimands}), only $48.3\%$ stay on the same kind of ground, and the largest off-diagonal flows all run toward Gateway concerns, which absorb $44.6\%$ of moves overall: $30\%$ of those leaving Platform ground, $43\%$ of those leaving Margin ground, and $33\%$ of those leaving Cul-de-sac ground. Movement between kinds of ground is therefore leaky rather than compartmentalized. The classes do not trap actors; they describe what a position costs to reach, and traffic runs downhill onto whatever is cheapest to enter from where an actor already stands. Adopted concerns then persist, with activity remaining high across subsequent years once a topic has been taken up (Supplementary Information \cref{fig:retention-curves}), so capacity accumulates rather than rotates. Entry, then, is governed by where an actor already stands and by who already holds the ground ahead. Whether members end up holding similar ground depends on what was on offer when each of them arrived.

\subsection{Who Can Raise What}

If everyone enters through the same permissive ground, the portfolios that result need not be alike, because what is available to enter changes as the archive fills. We compare each member's opening portfolio, its first 15 years of recorded specialization, against what the space offered during those same years, matching cohorts on career stage rather than calendar time so that a founder is not credited with an opportunity that did not yet exist. The comparison needs no class cut, since both sides can be stated on the two continuous scales the classes divide, and we report it that way first. The cohort joining 1991--2010 ($n=16$) built its opening portfolios out of disproportionately thin ground. Its mean holder breadth sits at $0.918\times$ of what the space offered in its opening years (95\% full-pipeline bootstrap interval $[0.874, 0.961]$) and its mean reach at $0.912\times$ ($[0.838, 1.007]$), where 1 is composition exactly in proportion to availability (\cref{fig:class-story}c; \texttt{scripts/cohort\_partition\_free.py}). The two earlier cohorts, 1961--80 ($n=14$) and 1981--90 ($n=12$), sit at parity on both scales ($0.99$ $[0.975, 1.011]$ and $1.00$ $[0.975, 1.018]$; $0.98$ $[0.952, 1.019]$ and $1.03$ $[0.967, 1.106]$). Intervals come from a full-pipeline bootstrap over 400 actor resamples that re-estimates each portfolio and the availability denominator together, since availability is itself estimated from the archive (Supplementary Information \cref{sec:cohort-method}). The reach interval for 1991--2010 just touches 1 at its upper end, so holder breadth, the quantity the entry analysis already identifies as the binding constraint, carries the clear deficit. Late arrivals entered through the same door as everyone else, but the ground they accumulated behind it was disproportionately the thinly connected kind: concerns reachable from few others, from which fewer onward moves are possible.

The same asymmetry appears in the classes' own terms (\cref{fig:class-story}c). The 1991--2010 cohort is over-represented on Margin ground ($1.44$, 95\% CI $[1.08, 1.76]$), the low-reach, narrow-holder kind, and under-represented on Platform ($0.46$ $[0.20, 0.78]$) and Cul-de-sac ground ($0.26$ $[0.00, 0.59]$), while Gateway ground is indistinguishable from parity ($1.19$ $[0.93, 1.47]$). The earlier cohorts are at parity on all four classes, with all eight intervals covering 1; the full set of twelve class and six continuous intervals is tabulated in Supplementary Information Table~\ref{tab:cohort-ratios}. Three qualifications bound this reading. It concerns one cohort: the two earlier cohorts are indistinguishable from each other and from parity. It does not depend on where the cuts fall: moving either median cut one rank, so that one boundary concern changes class on each axis, leaves the 1991--2010 ratios between $1.41$ and $1.44$ on Margin, $0.46$ and $0.48$ on Platform, and $0.13$ and $0.26$ on Cul-de-sac, none crossing parity (Supplementary Information \cref{sec:cohort-method}; \texttt{scripts/cohort\_boundary\_sensitivity.py}). Members joining after 2010 are excluded by construction, since their record cannot yet span the required 15 years. Part of the Platform and Cul-de-sac depression is definitional, because those classes are measured against the very members that help define them; rebuilding the classes from pre-1991 actors alone moves them to $0.66$ and $0.50$ while leaving Margin at $1.39$. The Margin elevation survives that redefinition. The comparison establishes that formally equal access to the agenda coexisted with an unequal composition of what could be built on it.

Every grouping in this paper carries the same caveat: actor position tracks tenure, with earlier entrants sitting further along the recovered ordering (Spearman $\rho=-0.58$, $p=9\times10^{-6}$), but position is also strongly tied to breadth ($\rho=0.74$), so tenure and capacity cannot be separated in this sample; actor positions are reported in full in Supplementary Information \cref{fig:portfolio_space_ridgelines} rather than relied on here. Partitioning schemes disagree even on how many groups exist (\cref{sec:partition-bootstrap,sec:class-validation}), while removing any single actor leaves the proximity matrix essentially unchanged ($r=0.99$; Methods). The underlying statement needs no partition. Net of prior attention, entry falls with the breadth of a concern's current holders, and it falls more steeply the further that concern lies from where an actor already stands. Which concerns sit on that thin ground, and who holds them, is the question the map can now answer.

\subsection{Concerns the Founding Instruments Never Named}\label{sec:environmental-reach}

Climate change never made it into the founding texts. Neither did biological prospecting, nor search and rescue as a named governance concern. That is not a small omission: concerns the founding instruments govern directly average a reach of $16.0$, against $9.3$ for concerns those documents merely reach (\cref{fig:space-by-instrument}), and all three orphaned concerns sit below the median reach of $14$, on Margin or Cul-de-sac ground (reach $7$, $12$, and $12$; \cref{fig:the_space_of_concerns}). By founding instruments we mean the Treaty System's constitutional layer, the 1959 Treaty, the 1991 Madrid Protocol and its six Annexes, and CCAMLR, as distinct from the Measures, Decisions, and Resolutions each meeting adopts. That layer has itself grown, from CCAMLR in 1980 to Annex VI in 2005, so ``founding'' names a constitutional tier rather than a text frozen in 1959, and the gap it leaves is current, not permanent. A fourth concern, Opening statements, has no home in that layer either, but it runs the other way: it sits at the center of the space with unusually many, unusually narrow holders, and we treat it as a procedural formality rather than a governance concern the instruments failed to name. Three substantive cases is a small comparison. It establishes how well connected these issues are, not how hard they are to enter, since reach carries only a small independent association with adoption once prior attention and distance are held constant. The gap is substantive, not a labelling accident: Article IX(1) of the 1959 Treaty enumerates six heads of competence, and tourism, environmental protection in general, and genetic resources appear in none of them. Tourism engagement has risen from the early 2000s all the same (Supplementary Information \cref{fig:upstream-decisions}), and Biological Prospecting, the genetic-resources frontier, sits on Margin ground (\cref{fig:space-by-instrument}). Where the Treaty spoke, the ground is well connected. Where it stayed silent, the ground is reachable from few others.

Half of the sixteen Climate Change holders, and four of the nine Prevention of marine pollution holders, joined after 1990. That is the same cohort the earlier comparison flagged as over-represented on Margin ground, prominent again among the holders of concerns the founding instruments never named. Drilling and Mineral resources run the other way: four of six Drilling holders and seven of eight Mineral resources holders joined before 1981, and the remaining two Drilling holders joined in the 1980s. No late entrant holds any of them. Every member arrived through the same formal route. The concerns they came to carry were not the same.

Marine Protected Areas shows where this logic stops. MPA sits on Platform ground, reachable from sixteen concerns (above the corpus median of $14$) and held by eleven actors, among them the broadest-portfolio incumbents: structurally, one of the easier environmental concerns to enter. Yet no new Southern Ocean MPA has been designated since the Ross Sea in 2016, despite sustained attention \cite{Brooks2019RossSea,CCAMLRMeasures2026}. Designation is CCAMLR's competence, and this space observes the Treaty-centered agenda, so that reading is bounded by what the space can see (\cref{sec:lowering-barriers}). For the least reachable concerns, the space locates the binding constraint. For MPA, the most reachable environmental concern, it shows that reach is not the constraint. That separation is exactly what a count cannot deliver. Whether the space also predicts where portfolios move next is the next test.

\subsection{The Space as an Instrument}

The remaining test is prediction, and the space passes it without fitting a single parameter. \Cref{fig:strength}a states the result directly: expansion events land nearer the prior portfolio than a size-matched redraw far more often than chance ($81\%$ $[79$--$84]$ of the 1,040 events in this panel, and $83.0\%$ of the 1,055 events in the displacement panel used above, Wilcoxon $p<10^{-15}$). A count records how much attention a concern receives; the space says which concerns lie within reach of an actor and why. Two properties distinguish it from that baseline. It is actor-specific: where a topic's raw popularity hands every Party the same ranking, the space conditions on each portfolio and issues one forecast per actor, about 18 distinct top-5 sets per period (\cref{fig:strength}b). And it carries the signal counting misses: counting ranks mainstream adoptions better, while the space ranks niche, non-mainstream adoptions better (\cref{fig:strength}c). In a consensus regime where attention converges on a shared core, a count should win on the mainstream; that the space wins the rest is what makes the two complementary rather than competing (Supplementary Information \cref{fig:density-prediction-oos}).

The space is a complement to counting, not a replacement: it supplies the actor-specific, adjacent component of where attention goes next, the component a count cannot represent. As a sufficiency check, a sequential rule built from these two features alone (retain part of the portfolio, adopt a few nearby concerns, allocate within the result) reproduces the record's breadth, topic popularity, and local-entry pattern, while full-reallocation and fixed-portfolio alternatives each fail on one of them (Supplementary Information \cref{fig:evolving-portfolio}).

\section{Discussion}
The documentary record of a consensus regime makes visible the process that unanimity otherwise keeps off the books. Counting and the space differ in kind, not in resolution. A count registers how much attention an issue receives. The space explains why attention can move from one concern to another, which no count can express. Formal unanimity compresses disagreement into official outcomes, which can make consensus institutions appear less political than they are; much of the consequential positioning occurs earlier, through agenda-setting, issue linkage, evidence production, and selective engagement \cite{BachrachBaratz1962,Stone2013,Vabulas2013,Steinberg2002,Drubel2023HiddenContestation}. Our claim concerns the visibility of that process, not actors' intentions.

The timing of landmark decisions shows the same pattern. Working papers are inputs to the consultative meetings: what actors submit is what gets discussed, so the record measures the agenda as it forms, years before any instrument is agreed. In all three of the system's landmark decisions, the discussion moved first (Supplementary Information \cref{fig:upstream-decisions}). The Madrid Protocol set jumps from about 4\% to about 22\% of the archive in 1986--90, before the 1991 signing; much of the jump is the CRAMRA controversy that the record anticipates alongside the Protocol that replaced it \cite{Joyner1998}. Liability engagement rises from zero after 1991, peaks in 1996--2000, and collapses once Annex VI is adopted in 2005; that annex has never entered into force, so it was the negotiation itself that exhausted the item. Tourism engagement rises from the early 2000s, before Measure 15 (2009). This is the shape that large-$N$ agenda studies describe as punctuated equilibrium \cite{BaumgartnerJones1993,JonesBaumgartner2005}, and the space supplies a structural account of the \emph{content} of bursts that counting alone cannot. The Madrid jump propagated into concerns adjacent to the activated minerals question, and exhaustion is a branch saturating, with nothing lying one step further \cite{Ocasio1997}. The space does not predict who carries a burst: a quasi-experimental probe of the 1991 shock finds that pre-shock adjacency does not predict who reoriented, so locality characterizes routine accumulation, not the response to exogenous shocks.

The results support a narrower claim than control over final decisions, and sustained attention does not imply agreement; the space records who keeps an issue present, not whether positions converge. What consensus does is relocate political structure upstream, into differences in which concerns actors can sustain and which adjacent concerns they can credibly enter. Counting-based readings of the same archive reach a compatible but differently shaped conclusion, describing a small and stable group of Parties that supplies most substantive papers \cite{DudeneyWalton2012,Sanchez2016}. We do not dispute that concentration; the relational view locates it. Concentration in volume becomes, on the space of concerns, a position: a set of held concerns with particular neighbors, and it is the neighborhood, not the volume, that determines which concerns lie one step away for whom. That is a shared arena with unequal positions, a view that complements work on governance for sustainability and networked forms of agency \cite{Agrawal2022,Rolfer2025b}. The cohort asymmetry is the sharpest form of the point, and also the most bounded: one cohort, descriptive, and unable to separate a space whose well-connected ground was already occupied from members who arrived with different capacities, including a sorting account in which capacity-constrained entrants were drawn to thinly connected ground rather than finding well-connected ground already taken.

These results describe a relational summary of joint specialization, not an external semantic ontology or a map that actors literally consult. Topics are nearby because actors repeatedly devote disproportionate attention to them together, so the geometry reflects both political behavior and the institution's own categories. The pooled space is a long-run summary, not a claim that issue relations remained fixed or that any actor knew the full topology at the time; occupation and edge intensity shift across decades on a common layout (Supplementary Information \cref{fig:decadal-space-stack,fig:space-ribbon-full}). We use the cumulative-lagged space for the temporal adoption test and reserve the pooled space for description and benchmarking.

What a relational view adds to agenda-setting theory is a constraint that the standard accounts leave implicit. Kingdon's coupling explains when a problem, a policy, and a politics stream meet, and the punctuated-equilibrium tradition explains why attention lurches between issue images and venues, but both treat the set of issues an actor could plausibly couple to as given. The space of concerns makes that set an object of measurement and shows it is neither universal nor fixed by capability alone. Two mechanisms could produce the pattern, and this design cannot separate them. Under a capability account, adjacent entry costs less because expertise, data, and drafting routines transfer. Under a coalition account, it costs less because the actors already sustaining a neighboring concern invite or co-sponsor. The holder-breadth result is the one piece of evidence that bears on the choice: a pure capability account predicts that what matters is the concern's position, while the data say it is the composition of its current holders, a fact about people rather than about skills. That points toward the coalition reading without establishing it, and distinguishing the two needs an institution where invitations or co-sponsorship intentions are observable.

The approach should travel as a measurement strategy rather than as an empirical regularity already established across consensus institutions. It suits settings with repeated agenda documents, stable or harmonized issue categories, and enough recurring actors to estimate co-specialization; it is less useful when agenda-setting occurs off record, labels change too quickly to align, or participation is sparse. Nor does it replace a model of consensus formation or regime effectiveness \cite{Keohane1984,Young2011Effectiveness}. Antarctic Measures, Decisions, and Resolutions still depend on legal procedure, consultative status, drafting coalitions, and timing; the space identifies the terrain those processes operate on, not a mechanical path from specialization to adoption. The natural next cases are regimes with the same documentary habits (CCAMLR, the Arctic Council, the UNFCCC submission record), and the validation that would settle the question is specific: apply the pipeline where roll-call votes or independent position estimates exist, and test whether the recovered space predicts them \cite{Orsini2019,Baturo2017}. Within this archive, the obvious next step is the decision layer itself, so that whose concerns become Measures can be asked about the same object and in the same vocabulary.

Formal statements and final decisions omit the slower reorganization of institutional attention. Repeated co-specialization provides a way to observe that process and extends product-space reasoning from economies to governance \cite{Hidalgo2007,Hidalgo2018}. Environmental change, scientific development, and geopolitical pressure are altering both the Antarctic policy environment and the role of expertise within it \cite{Chown2013,Solomon2016,Holland2020,Nature2018ReformATS,Turekian2025ScienceDiplomacy}. The concerns those pressures raise are disproportionately the ones the founding instruments never named, and those sit on the thinnest ground the space contains (\cref{sec:environmental-reach}); an institution absorbing new environmental problems works against its own topology, not just against inertia. Two practical questions follow: whether institutions can lower the cost of entering adjacent concerns, and whether transparency can keep strategically sensitive scientific capabilities legible without imputing intent.

\subsection{Lowering barriers to agenda participation}\label{sec:lowering-barriers}
The first of these questions has a concrete target in this archive. Actors enter concerns through ground they already sustain, and the ground that stays reachable at distance is the ground narrow portfolios already hold, which makes unequal agenda capacity a practical institutional problem. For non-consultative parties, the formal path to decision rights runs through Article IX(2), which ties consultative status to substantial scientific research activity \cite{AntarcticTreaty1959}; the document record measured here is part of what counts toward that upgrade, and the science gate structuring it has been described as a hierarchy \cite{Yao2021}. Among Consultative Parties, formally equal consensus rights coexist with different portfolio positions. The analysis does not show that information or drafting costs cause these differences; it identifies a mechanism that can be tested. The participation question also has a history outside the arena, in the 1980s challenge led by Malaysia at the United Nations General Assembly over who is in the room at all \cite{UNGA1983Antarctica,Dodds2010}. Lowering barriers within the arena does not answer that.

Intervention should focus on the infrastructure through which observations become documents and documents become recognized concerns. This refines rather than replaces earlier prescriptions, since levelling the participation field has been argued for on the basis of submission counts \cite{Sanchez2016}, and adjacency sharpens the target from volume to position. Building on existing recommendatory reporting support \cite{ATCMResolution4_2026}, a pilot could combine joint monitoring, co-sponsored working papers, and technical assistance in document preparation, with pre-specified outcomes testing whether participants sustain concerns across windows or enter adjacent concerns earlier. It would formalize existing practice rather than import a new mechanism, since mixed consultative and non-consultative pairs already co-occupy adjacent parts of the space (Supplementary Information \cref{fig:regime-pair-institutional}), and it is timely as Parties develop a framework for tourism and non-governmental activities \cite{IAATO2026Vessel,ATCM48Outcomes2026} and as the BBNJ Agreement raises questions about how biodiversity governance beyond national jurisdiction will interlock with the Antarctic regime \cite{UNBBNJ2026,Wang2024Bioprospecting}. Better information infrastructure will not democratize governance by itself, since its effects depend on who defines categories and converts information into recognized interventions \cite{Kloppenburg2022}; the stalled MPA record discussed above (\cref{sec:environmental-reach}) is a reminder that agenda presence does not guarantee outcomes, and that the bottleneck may be political will \cite{Moallemi2025a}.

The second question concerns the resource-science corridor, where drilling, sub-glacial lakes, and marine acoustics have clear scientific purposes while the associated knowledge and infrastructure may also matter to future resource or strategic questions \cite{Sampaio2022,HansenMagnusson2022,Szpak2025}. The co-specialization pattern identifies that adjacency; it does not reveal motives, establish covert dual-use activity, or show science to be conflict in disguise. Transparency there is a question for institutional assessment rather than a conclusion of this analysis, and any proposal would need to apply the same criteria to comparable activities by all Parties. The map can identify where scrutiny may be useful; it cannot determine intent or substitute for legal and political analysis.

\subsection{Conclusion}
Formal equality in decision rules can coexist with unequal capacity to sustain concerns. The space of concerns makes that difference observable before final decisions are taken. It shows where actors sit, which concerns lie within reach of their existing portfolios, and where institutional bottlenecks may arise. Shared monitoring, drafting support, and actor-neutral transparency are hypotheses for addressing those bottlenecks, not effects established by this study. Institutional adaptation depends on formal rights, but it also depends on who can connect an emerging problem to sustained institutional attention.

\section{Groupings and Their Validation}

Three ways of grouping this space appear in the paper: the three engagement modes used for descriptive actor positioning (\cref{sec:mode-construction}), the four concern classes used in the main text, and network-native communities on the proximity graph. Each is fixed by a stated criterion, an elbow on the inertia curve for the modes and a median split on two frozen structural quantities for the classes, and none of those criteria speaks to whether the resulting assignment is reproducible. This part asks that second question of all three schemes, by the same actor bootstrap in each case, and reaches the same answer every time.

\subsection{Bootstrap stability of the mode partition}\label{sec:partition-bootstrap}
Selecting $k$ and reproducing an assignment are different questions, and the elbow criterion in \cref{sec:mode-construction} addresses only the first. To address the second we resample the 66 actors with replacement and rebuild the entire pipeline on each resampled panel, meaning RPA, proximity, the shortest-path distance matrix, the one-dimensional classical-MDS ordering, and the weighted $k$-means zones, then compare the recovered partition with the observed one. Because classical MDS fixes an axis only up to sign, each replicate is oriented to the observed axis before zone identities are compared; agreement between partitions is measured by the adjusted Rand index, which is invariant to how zones are numbered. Nine of 200 replicates isolated at least one topic, making the shortest-path distance infinite and the MDS ordering undefined, and were discarded rather than repaired, since a disconnected space is not a noisier estimate of the same object.

The mode partition does not reproduce. Across the 191 usable replicates the adjusted Rand index averages $0.239$ (median $0.234$; 90\% interval $[0.049, 0.467]$), and topics return to their observed zone in $58.5\%$ of resamples against a zone-size-matched chance baseline of $36.9\%$. Only $13.3\%$ of topics keep their zone in at least $90\%$ of replicates, and $20.0\%$ in at least $75\%$. The least stable topics are those sitting near a boundary, among them Human Footprint and Wilderness Values ($0.20$), Environmental Impact Assessment ($0.20$), CEP Strategy Discussions ($0.21$), Site Guidelines for Visitors ($0.23$), and Environmental Protection General ($0.24$), while topics deep inside a zone are recovered reliably.

One might suspect that the failure lies in the interval scheme rather than in the space, since the filtered backbone drawn in \cref{fig:the_space_of_concerns} plainly shows structure, and communities on that graph should be topological rather than intervals on a line. We therefore repeated the same bootstrap with two network-native partitions, greedy modularity communities on the full proximity graph and on the Figure~1 backbone (maximum-spanning tree plus the strongest 5\% of edges). Neither rescues reproducibility, with adjusted Rand indices of $0.30$ for the full graph, which collapses to two communities (the procedural core against everything peripheral), and $0.26$ for the backbone, which fragments into seven, against $0.24$ for the interval scheme. The three schemes do not even agree on how many groups exist (3, 2, and 7). The filtered map does show structure, and its branches are precisely what backbone modularity finds, but that structure is a cored continuum with radiating branches, not a set of exchangeable blocks, and no partition of it reproduces under actor resampling. \texttt{scripts/partition\_scheme\_comparison.py} reproduces this comparison.

This result is narrower than it first appears, and it should be read precisely. It is not evidence that the space of concerns is unstable, since the geometry is highly robust, and removing any single actor leaves the proximity matrix essentially unchanged (mean off-diagonal $r=0.99$; Methods). What is unstable is the operation of cutting a smooth one-dimensional ordering into three contiguous blocks, which relocates boundary topics whenever the axis shifts slightly. That is the expected behavior of a partition imposed on a continuum, and it is why the locality results in the main text are estimated without any partition, why the mode percentages are reported as a readable restatement of the partition-free paired test rather than as independent evidence, and why we describe the modes as a vocabulary for reading the map rather than as groups discovered in the data. \texttt{scripts/mode\_partition\_bootstrap.py} reproduces these numbers.

\subsection{Reproducibility of the four concern classes}\label{sec:class-validation}
The concern classes used in \cref{fig:the_space_of_concerns,fig:class-story} are a different construction from the modes, cutting two structural quantities at their medians rather than dividing a one-dimensional ordering, so they require their own test, and they are held to the same standard. A concern's \emph{reach} is the number of other concerns within $\phi \ge 0.30$ of it; its \emph{holder breadth} is the mean portfolio size of the actors specialized in it. Holder breadth is the second iteration of the Method of Reflections \cite{HidalgoHausmann2009}. Where a concern's ubiquity counts how many actors hold it, holder breadth averages how diversified those holders are. The two are negatively related by construction in that framework, and they are here (Spearman $\rho=-0.79$), because members with wide portfolios are disproportionately the ones sustaining concerns that few others hold. Holder breadth therefore carries information about rarity as well as about capacity, which is why the entry model below controls for prior attention. No monotone association between the two is detectable across the 45 concerns (Spearman $\rho = +0.005$, bootstrap 95\% interval $[-0.22,+0.43]$; distance correlation $0.34$, so they are non-redundant rather than independent), which is why the median split yields four populated classes rather than a diagonal. Both medians are computed once on the aggregate archive and frozen, so a concern cannot change class between figures; \texttt{concern\_classes.py} is the single definition used by every panel.

Subjected to the same actor bootstrap as the modes, resampling the 66 actors with replacement, rebuilding RPA and proximity, and reclassifying, the classes reproduce at an adjusted Rand index of $0.30$ (median $0.297$, mean $0.302$, 90\% interval $[0.157, 0.462]$ over 300 replicates), with $36\%$ of concerns keeping their class in at least three-quarters of replicates and $9\%$ in at least nine-tenths. This is marginally better than the three-mode partition ($0.24$) and no more reproducible in any meaningful sense. The least stable concerns are again those near a boundary, among them Tourism and NG\_Activities ($13\%$), CEP Strategy Discussions ($19\%$), and Liability ($24\%$). Moving the reach threshold rather than resampling actors is less damaging, with agreement against the $\phi\ge0.30$ scheme of $0.49$, $0.67$, $0.83$, and $0.59$ at thresholds of $0.20$, $0.25$, $0.35$, and $0.40$, and class sizes staying within two concerns of $(11, 13, 11, 10)$ throughout.

We therefore draw the same conclusion for the classes as for the modes, and for the same reason, since a median cut of a continuum relocates its boundary cases whenever the underlying quantities shift slightly. The classes are a vocabulary for reading what a position in the space affords, not a claim that Antarctic concerns come in four kinds. Two things follow for how they are used. First, no result in the main text rests on the exact membership of a class, because \cref{fig:class-story}a is a distance gradient that holds within all four, and the entry asymmetry in \cref{fig:the_space_of_concerns}c and \cref{fig:class-story}c is a statement about reach and holder breadth that survives any relabelling preserving those two quantities. Second, the underlying claim can be stated with no partition at all, since entry rises with a concern's reach and falls with its holders' breadth, and the four-way cut exists to make that statement legible, not to establish it. \texttt{scripts/validate\_quadrants.py} reproduces these numbers.

\subsection{Exploratory finer partition}
The elbow criterion that fixes the modes at $k=3$ also admits a useful but more descriptive five-zone partition, reported here for completeness. At $k=5$, the snapped centers fall on Exchange of Information, Cooperation with Other Organisations, Area Protection and Management Plans, Operation of the Committee for Environmental Protection (CEP), and the State of the Antarctic Environment Report (SAER), producing topic blocks of sizes 8, 15, 12, 7, and 3. Substantively, this split refines the three-mode summary by separating institutional information exchange from operational-environmental management, distinguishing area-protection and access governance from the central compliance block, and isolating a small frontier-assessment tail around Sub-glacial Lakes, SAER, and Environmental Domains Analysis. A six-zone partition adds further detail by splitting the left-hand coordination region and the right-hand frontier region, but it leaves a very small two-topic residual block (SAER and Environmental Domains Analysis) and reduces the interpretability of actor anchoring. We therefore treat the five-zone solution as an exploratory reading of substructure within the broader modes, reported here rather than in the main text, while retaining three modes as the main minimum adequate summary used for actor positioning, transition analysis, and complementarity tests. Nothing above suggests that the five-zone cut would reproduce any better than the three-zone one; it is offered as a description, not as a more stable alternative.

\section{Methods and Data}
\subsection{Data Description and Processing}
We analyze working papers and information documents submitted to the Antarctic Treaty System (ATS) from 1961 to 2025. Throughout the paper, ``ATS'' denotes the broader Antarctic governance complex anchored in the Antarctic Treaty and related instruments, but the empirical corpus used here comes from the Treaty-centered document archive rather than the full documentary output of every ATS institution. All documents in the corpus were submitted to Antarctic Treaty Consultative Meetings (ATCMs); the Secretariat's harmonized taxonomy also covers topics handled by the Committee for Environmental Protection (CEP), whose matters reach the ATCM agenda, and meeting volume rises partly for institutional reasons, since ATCMs moved from biennial to annual in the 1990s and the CEP has met annually since 1998. Conference Room Papers, the informal negotiation layer of the meetings, are not systematically present in the public archive and are not in the corpus, so the space observes agenda preparation rather than negotiation text. The corpus was compiled from the official ATS document archive and enriched with thematic labels, submitting parties, and document types \cite{Yaryab2024}. Documents submitted by the ATS Secretariat were retained for completeness. The Secretariat therefore plays two roles in the corpus by assigning the harmonized category labels for all documents while also appearing as one submitting actor among the 66; the labels are applied uniformly across submitters, and Secretariat submissions enter the analysis on the same terms as any other actor.

Data collection was performed on 6 February 2026 from the ATS website (\url{https://www.ats.aq/devAS/Meetings/DocDatabase?lang=e}) using the antarctic-database-go collector by Carlo Hamalainen (\url{https://github.com/carlohamalainen/antarctic-database-go}). The framework supports reproducibility by storing cached Hypertext Transfer Protocol (HTTP) responses and local copies of retrieved source documents, recording processing status in SQLite, and enabling deterministic re-runs and audits of the extraction pipeline. If the website changes or documents are removed, the collected dataset can therefore be reconstructed from the cached responses, allowing the analysis to be replicated even as the source data evolves.

The category labels are Secretariat-assigned (not natural language processing (NLP)-derived) and thus the space reflects co-participation within the Secretariat's own issue taxonomy rather than semantic similarity. Because agenda items vary across meetings over time, the Secretariat created cross-temporal categories to provide a harmonized, consistent classification of documents across the archive. We use that harmonized topic system directly and will archive the category crosswalk used in the analysis together with the processed panel and code release. For preprocessing, we expanded multi-party entries into one row per submitting actor and deduplicated attachment-linked records using unique \texttt{paper\_id}, yielding 6,591 unique papers across 1961--2025. We also excluded the 18 papers (0.3\% of the deduplicated corpus, all after 2010) whose only label was the placeholder category \texttt{ALL} or \texttt{Other}, leaving 6,573 papers with substantive topic assignments; no retained paper mixed placeholder and substantive labels. Every retained paper carries exactly one substantive label, so no document contributes to more than one actor--topic cell and administrative co-classification within a document is not a channel through which labels become coupled in the record. \subsection{Constructing the Space of Concerns}
Before the construction itself, it is worth stating precisely what this measure sees and what it cannot. Counting and co-specialization are differently blind rather than better and worse versions of one another. Where participation in a concern is nearly universal, counting registers a link but co-specialization cannot, because no actor is disproportionately active in it; conversely, where a small community concentrates on a few technical concerns, co-specialization registers a link even though raw overlap stays low. Counting therefore recovers common ground and co-specialization recovers specialist corridors. We estimate the second, and accept the cost that the relational view is least informative precisely where every Party participates, which is why the descriptive claims below concern the structure of specialization, not the salience of an issue.

Let $x(a,i)$ denote the number of submissions by actor $a$ on topic $i$, where actors include consultative parties, non-consultative parties, observers, relevant organizations, and the ATS Secretariat whenever they appear as submitters in the archive. We define the normalized activity share
\begin{equation}\label{eq:activity_share}
\pi(a,i) = \frac{x(a,i)}{\sum_j x(a,j)},
\end{equation}

and the system-wide importance of topic $i$ as

\begin{equation}\label{eq:topic_importance}
Z_i = \frac{\sum_c x(c,i)}{\sum_{d,j} x(d,j)}.
\end{equation}

The Revealed Policy Advantage (RPA; a revealed comparative advantage (RCA)-style specialization index) is then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rpa}
 \rho(a,i) = \frac{\pi(a,i)}{Z_i}.
\end{equation}
We use the label \emph{revealed policy advantage} rather than the standard
\emph{revealed comparative advantage} to mark the shift from trade to governance.
The mathematics is unchanged because this is the same relative-specialization ratio
used in revealed-comparative-advantage work, relabeled for actors and policy topics rather than
countries and products. The analogy has a limit worth stating. In trade the index is informative because export performance has survived a competitive filter; submissions face no such filter, since actors submit to signal, to comply, or to shape outcomes, and nothing separates capacity from intent. RPA therefore reveals attention backed by drafting and scientific capacity, not unexercised capability or preference. The institution does apply a selection of its own, since consultative status is tied to substantial scientific research activity under Article IX(2) of the Treaty, so the filter that operates here falls precisely on the scientific capacity the index reads. There is also no analogue of the product-space value ordering, and concerns cannot be ranked by complexity or yield, and the policy goal here, wider participation, does not require such a ranking. Co-specialization remains informative under the weaker reading, because when the same actors repeatedly devote disproportionate attention to two topics together, the apparatus that produces one is available for the other, whatever it is deployed for.

Topics are linked by proximity
\begin{equation}\label{eq:proximity}
\phi(i,j)
=
\min\!\left\{
\begin{aligned}
 &\Pr\!\bigl(\rho(a,i) > 1 \mid \rho(a,j) > 1\bigr),\\
 &\Pr\!\bigl(\rho(a,j) > 1 \mid \rho(a,i) > 1\bigr)
\end{aligned}
\right\}.
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
 \Pr(\rho(a,j)>1 \mid \rho(a,i)>1) =\frac{\sum_a \mathbf{1}[\rho(a,i)>1 \land \rho(a,j)>1]}
 {\sum_a \mathbf{1}[\rho(a,i)>1]},
\end{equation}
yielding a weighted topic network. Taking the minimum of the two conditional probabilities serves three purposes. First, using conditional rather than joint probability controls for differences in topic prevalence, allowing two relatively rare topics to register as close if specialization in one reliably coincides with specialization in the other, whereas a joint probability would mechanically shrink with rarity. Second, taking the minimum prevents one-way dependence from overstating proximity. If a niche topic almost always appears alongside a broad topic, ($P(\text{broad}\mid \text{niche})$) may be high even when the reverse is low; the minimum forces the proximity to be limited by that weaker direction. Third, this yields a symmetric measure of relatedness, so the link between topics reflects mutual co-specialization rather than directional inclusion. Probabilities are estimated empirically across members within the relevant analysis window. Given the 66-actor set, each non-zero proximity rests on a modest number of co-specialized actors (the strongest tie in \cref{fig:the_space_of_concerns}d rests on 12 of 66), so the geometry is a coarse aggregate of co-specialization rather than a high-resolution estimate of relatedness.
We construct both an aggregate space (for the mode-wide map) and time-resolved spaces by rebuilding proximities within each time window, allowing the topology itself to evolve rather than assuming a fixed structure over time; \cref{fig:space-ribbon-full} visualizes the actor-level support behind these time-resolved proximities as it shifts across windows.
This adapts the product-space proximity logic from economic complexity to governance concerns \cite{Hidalgo2007}.

\subsection{Constructing Modes from the Concern Space}\label{sec:mode-construction}
The main text relies on the four concern classes (\cref{sec:class-validation}) rather than on a partition of actors, and reports a three-mode summary of actor position only as a descriptive aid (\cref{fig:portfolio_space_ridgelines}). For completeness, that summary is built by ordering topics along the one-dimensional classical-MDS reduction of the shortest-path distance matrix, locating three zones where excess engagement concentrates by weighted $k$-means, and assigning each topic to the nearest zone center. The ordering axis carries $23.8\%$ of the eigenvalue mass and shows no eigengap at three clusters, and $k=3$ is chosen by an elbow on a smooth inertia curve, so the division is interpretive rather than discovered. The partition does not reproduce under actor resampling (\cref{sec:partition-bootstrap}), which is why no result in the paper rests on it. For descriptive mode-position summaries we retain actors with at least three specialized topics in the aggregate archive; \texttt{scripts/mode\_partition\_bootstrap.py} reproduces the assignment and its instability.

\subsection{Hazard Model for Local Topic Adoption}
To test whether new specialization is locally concentrated in the space of concerns, we estimate a rolling actor-level hazard model on adjacent year windows. For each actor $a$, topic $i$, and transition $t-1 \rightarrow t$, an observation is included only if the topic is at risk:
\[
\text{at-risk}_{a i t}=1 \iff \rho_{a i, t-1} < 1.
\]
The binary outcome is then
\[
y_{a i t}=1 \iff \rho_{a i, t} \ge 1 \ \text{and}\ \rho_{a i, t-1} < 1.
\]
Let the previously active support be
\[
S_{a,t-1}=\{j:\rho_{aj,t-1}\ge 1\},
\]
let the at-risk set be
\[
R_{at}=\{i:\rho_{ai,t-1}<1\},
\]
and let $m_{at}=\sum_{i\in R_{at}} y_{ait}$ denote the number of newly adopted topics in an actor-period. Our main locality specification is then a 5-year actor-period conditional logit,
\begin{dmath}
\Pr(\mathbf{y}_{at}\mid m_{at}, X_{at})
\propto
\exp\!\left(
\sum_{i\in R_{at}}
y_{ait}\bigl(
\beta_d d_{a i, t-1}
\!+\!
\beta_p \mathrm{pop}_{i,t-1}
\bigr)
\right),
\end{dmath}
where
\[
d_{a i, t-1}=1-\max_{j\in S_{a,t-1}}\phi(i,j)
\]
is the raw distance from candidate topic $i$ to the actor's prior portfolio and $\mathrm{pop}_{i,t-1}$ is prior topic popularity (share of actors with $\mathrm{RPA} \ge 1$). The model conditions on the total number of adoption events $m_{at}$ within each actor-period and on an actor-period-specific intercept, so identification comes from which at-risk topics are selected inside a given choice set rather than from cross-panel differences in baseline adoption rates. Actor-periods with no prior support or no new adoptions therefore do not contribute to the likelihood, and prior breadth is absorbed into the conditioned actor-period effect rather than estimated as a separate slope.
In the main specification, this leaves 1,055 actor-period choice sets and 40,129 at-risk topic observations, with $\beta_{\mathrm{distance}}=-2.93$ (SE $0.18$; 95\% CI $[-3.27, -2.59]$). Because the rolling windows overlap, successive choice sets for the same actor are not statistically independent and the naive intervals do not adjust for this dependence. We therefore re-estimated the effect under four designs that do. An actor-level bootstrap, resampling the 66 actors with replacement over 200 replicates, absorbs within-actor overlap into the sampling distribution and returns the same interval (OR per $+0.1$ of $0.75$, 95\% bootstrap CI $[0.72, 0.77]$). A panel built on non-overlapping 5-year windows, in which choice sets share no documents and the dependence is removed by construction, gives OR $0.79$ $[0.76, 0.82]$ on 328 choice sets. Restricting the risk sets to the 42 states gives OR $0.76$ $[0.73, 0.78]$, so mandate-driven expert bodies are not carrying the effect. Adding a topic-age control, which addresses the objection that old topics are both more proximate and more likely to be entered, leaves the estimate at OR $0.74$ $[0.72, 0.77]$. \texttt{scripts/hazard\_robustness\_dependence.py} reproduces all four. The distance effect is also stable across all three space constructions (\cref{fig:hazard-space-sensitivity}). Distances are computed from a cumulative-lagged space of concerns built using only information available up to $t-1$ and kept on the raw $1-\phi$ scale. This choice is intentional because the cumulative-lagged space provides the strictest prior-information version of the locality test while raw $1-\phi$ remains bounded, directly interpretable, and aligned with the positive $\phi$-fit term used in the generative evolving-portfolio model below. \cref{fig:hazard-space-sensitivity} compares the same conditional-logit specification under aggregate full-history and instantaneous previous-window spaces. A log transformation $-\log \phi$ is more natural for longer-range connectivity, but in these data the mass of zero-proximity ties creates a long right tail in the non-aggregate spaces, so the primary hazard analysis stays on raw $1-\phi$.

\subsection{Cohort Uptake Relative to Opportunity}\label{sec:cohort-method}
\Cref{fig:class-story}c compares what each cohort of members built its opening portfolio out of against what the space made available while they were building it. Comparing cohorts at the same calendar moment would credit a founder with opportunities that did not yet exist, so we match on career stage instead.

An actor is eligible if at least $\mathrm{WIN}=15$ years separate its first and last appearance in the archive; the requirement excludes members joining after 2010 by construction, since their record cannot yet span the window. Its \emph{opening window} runs from its first recorded year to that year plus $\mathrm{WIN}-1$. Within that window we rebuild the actor-topic interaction matrix from the submissions falling inside it, compute RPA exactly as for the pooled space, and treat a concern as held when $\mathrm{RPA}\ge 1$. Actors holding fewer than three specialized concerns in their own opening window are dropped, since class shares are not meaningful over one or two concerns; this leaves 14 members joining 1961--80, 12 joining 1981--90, and 16 joining 1991--2010, against 17, 13, and 17 that clear the tenure test alone.

The same comparison can be stated without any cut. The four classes divide two continuous quantities, and the uptake-to-opportunity logic above compares each cohort's mean holder breadth and mean reach against the availability-weighted mean of those quantities in its opening window, where availability weights each concern by its share of the window's recorded specialization. The 1991--2010 cohort sits at $0.918$ of availability on holder breadth (95\% full-pipeline bootstrap interval $[0.874, 0.961]$) and $0.912$ on reach ($[0.838, 1.007]$); the two earlier cohorts sit at $0.99$/$1.00$ and $0.98$/$1.03$ respectively, statistically indistinguishable from parity on both scales. The reach interval's upper end just touches 1, so the reach deficit is the softer of the two; holder breadth, the quantity the entry analysis already identifies as the binding constraint, carries the clear deficit. \texttt{scripts/cohort\_partition\_free.py} reproduces the continuous restatement.

For the legible four-class version of the same comparison, \emph{availability} is the share of all specializations recorded in a window, across all actors, that fall in each of the four concern classes. An actor's uptake-to-opportunity ratio for a class is then its own share of that class divided by the availability share, so a value of 1 means its portfolio is composed in exactly the proportions on offer at the time. Cohort values are the unweighted mean of member ratios. Availability is estimated from the same archive rather than known, so an interval that resamples actors while holding it fixed would be too narrow. We therefore bootstrap the full pipeline, so that each of 400 replicates resamples the 66 actors with replacement and re-estimates both the member portfolios and the availability denominator inside the replicate, and intervals are the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the resulting cohort means. Because numerator and denominator move together under resampling, this is both more honest and tighter than the fixed-denominator alternative. The classes themselves are held at their frozen pooled definitions throughout (\cref{sec:class-validation}), so a concern cannot change class between replicates. Rebuilding the classes from pre-1991 actors alone, so that the 1991--2010 cohort cannot help define the ground it is measured against, leaves the Margin elevation at $1.39$ while moving Platform to $0.66$ and Cul-de-sac to $0.50$. \texttt{fig02\_entry\_and\_cohorts.py} reproduces the panel and prints every interval.

Nor do these ratios depend on where the two median cuts fall. Moving either cut one rank, so that one boundary concern changes class on each axis, leaves the 1991--2010 ratios on the same side of parity throughout, between $1.41$ and $1.44$ on Margin, $0.46$ and $0.48$ on Platform, $0.13$ and $0.26$ on Cul-de-sac, and at or above $1.19$ on Gateway. \texttt{scripts/cohort\_boundary\_sensitivity.py} reproduces these numbers. All twelve class intervals and all six continuous intervals are tabulated in Table~\ref{tab:cohort-ratios}.

\subsection{Sequential Evolving-Portfolio Model of Portfolio Dynamics}
The main text uses this only as a sufficiency check, so it is stated briefly. The rule proceeds in three stages per window. It retains a fraction of the current portfolio, adopt a small number of additional concerns with probability increasing in $\phi$-proximity to what is retained, and allocate submission volume across the result. Parameters are fixed at values matching the observed retention rate and mean number of adoptions per window rather than fitted to the outcomes being reproduced. Run forward from the 1961 state, the rule reproduces the record's mean breadth ($3.35$ observed against $3.32$ simulated), topic popularity ($2.45$ against $2.45$), and mean entry rank ($0.677$ against $0.675$), while a full-reallocation variant fails on breadth and a fixed-portfolio variant fails on entry rank (\cref{fig:evolving-portfolio}). Because the adoption stage already contains a proximity term, this demonstrates that locality plus retention is \emph{sufficient} to generate the coarse structure of the archive; it is not independent confirmation that locality holds. Robustness to archive thinning is reported in \cref{fig:split-support-thinning}.

We also tested whether locality findings depend on how the space of concerns is constructed. \Cref{fig:hazard-space-sensitivity} compares the same 5-year conditional-logit hazard under three definitions of the space of concerns, namely aggregate full-history, instantaneous previous-window, and cumulative-lagged (all data up to $t-1$), always using raw distance $d=1-\max\phi$ to the prior portfolio. The prior-topic-popularity control is also positive in all three specifications (aggregate $\beta_{\mathrm{pop}}=7.59$, standard error (SE) $=0.30$; instantaneous $6.00$, $0.37$; cumulative-lagged $7.67$, $0.30$), so the distance effect is not simply absorbing generic attraction to already popular topics. We keep raw $1-\phi$ as the primary scale because it is bounded and aligns directly with the positive $\phi$-fit term in the evolving-portfolio model. One check concerns how entry is defined rather than how the space is built. Entry is defined as an actor's Revealed Policy Advantage crossing from below to above 1, and because RPA is a share ratio an actor can cross that threshold when a topic's system-wide share falls, without changing its own behavior. We therefore rebuilt the outcome on absolute activity, counting an entry only where the actor filed no paper on the topic in the prior window and at least one in the next, and re-estimated on the same 79,207 at-risk observations with the same popularity control. The two definitions disagree on roughly a third of events (1,287 of 1,871 relative entries are also absolute entries; 584 are relative only and 466 absolute only), yet the gradient is almost identical, at an odds ratio of $0.582$ per $+0.1$ of distance under the relative definition and $0.607$ under the absolute one (both $p<10^{-15}$, member-clustered). The point estimates differ in level from the headline conditional-logit figure of $0.75$ because this check is a member-clustered logistic on the full at-risk set rather than a model conditioning on the number of adoptions within each actor-period choice set; what matters for the check is that the two entry definitions agree within the same specification. Locality is therefore not an artifact of the ratio. \texttt{scripts/entry\_definition\_robustness.py} reproduces this.

The entry-barrier reading is likewise stable under the two construction choices it inherits from the entry panel: the RPA threshold that defines an actor as specialized, and the topic list itself. Moving the threshold to $0.8$ or $1.2$ moves the conditional-logit holder-breadth odds ratio only from $0.89$ to $0.88$ and $0.88$, leaves reach at $1.05$ and $1.06$, and keeps the breadth $\times$ distance interaction near $0.85$ ($0.85$ and $0.85$); the pooled full-at-risk model behaves the same way. Dropping the two procedural topics, Opening statements and Exchange of information, from the candidate space removes $3{,}704$ at-risk observations but leaves the same three quantities essentially unchanged (holder breadth $0.88$, reach $1.05$, interaction $0.82$). \texttt{scripts/entry\_barrier\_sensitivity.py} reproduces all three variants and writes every coefficient to \texttt{output/entry\_barrier\_sensitivity.csv}.

Two further checks target mechanical concerns in the proximity construction itself. First, each actor's own submission history contributes to the proximities used to compute its distances, which could in principle build a self-fulfilling element into the locality test. We therefore recomputed all distances leave-one-actor-out, so that for every actor, the space of concerns is re-estimated with that actor's submissions removed before RPA and proximity estimation, holding the outcome panel fixed. The actor-excluded spaces are nearly identical to the full space (mean off-diagonal correlation $0.99$), and the locality effect attenuates only modestly while remaining strong (cumulative-lagged $\beta_{\mathrm{distance}}=-2.57$, 95\% CI $[-2.90, -2.24]$, odds ratio per $0.1$ distance $0.77$; aggregate $\beta=-3.36$). Second, full counting credits a co-sponsored paper once to every sponsor, which mechanically generates co-specialization among frequent co-sponsors. Fractional attribution is the established remedy in this archive, and \textcite{DudeneyWalton2012} adopt it as their primary counting scheme for Antarctic working papers, with additional weight given to the first-named Party and separate treatment of the two meeting venues. We use the simpler even split, since the first-named convention is not consistently applied across the full 1961--2025 span, and treat the result as a robustness check rather than as the primary construction. Rebuilding the space with fractional counting, in which each paper contributes $1/n$ to each of its $n$ sponsors, changes the aggregate proximity matrix appreciably (off-diagonal correlation with the full-count space $r=0.84$) but leaves the locality estimate essentially unchanged (cumulative-lagged $\beta_{\mathrm{distance}}=-2.84$, 95\% CI $[-3.18, -2.50]$; aggregate $\beta=-3.53$). Local adoption in the space of concerns is therefore not an artifact of actors' self-contribution to the proximity estimates, nor of co-sponsorship inflation.

A third check concerns what the space means, not whether it is stable. No edge is computed from text, so the space is not a semantic construction by definition; but because the topic labels are the Secretariat's own natural-language phrases, the recovered geometry could still happen to track how those phrases are worded. We test that coincidence directly, comparing the proximity matrix against a TF-IDF cosine similarity computed over the same 45 labels, under both word (1--2 gram, English stop words removed) and character (3--5 gram, word-boundary) analyzers with sublinear term-frequency scaling. Significance is assessed by a Mantel permutation with $10{,}000$ joint row-and-column shuffles, which preserves the dependence among the $990$ pairwise entries that invalidates an ordinary correlation test. Lexical similarity is positively but weakly associated with proximity, explaining $1.2\%$ of its variance for word $n$-grams ($r=0.111$, Spearman $\rho=0.079$, $p=0.002$) and $1.8\%$ for character $n$-grams ($r=0.134$, $\rho=0.153$, $p=0.001$). The residual association is what shared institutional vocabulary would produce, since many labels contain ``Antarctic'' or ``environmental'', and it leaves the geometry essentially unexplained by language. A lexical rather than neural baseline is deliberate, so that the comparison requires no external model and reproduces exactly; it also tests the objection in its strongest practical form, since the labels are short administrative phrases whose overlap is mostly literal. \texttt{scripts/semantic\_baseline.py} reproduces these numbers and writes the full pair table.

\subsection{Robustness of the Concern Space}
The space of concerns itself can be stress-tested by removing actors prior to construction and asking how much the resulting topic-topic proximity matrix changes. We proceed greedily by removing, at each step, the actor whose exclusion produces the largest root mean squared difference from the current full-space proximity matrix, then repeating the procedure. This does not test a substantive behavioral claim directly; it asks whether the shared topology of concerns is jointly produced or dominated by a small number of actors.

Low direct overlap between two states also does not imply that they sit far apart in the space, since many sovereign-state pairs share few specialized concerns yet occupy adjacent, mutually supportive regions (\cref{fig:portfolio_space_ridgelines}b), which is why portfolio overlap and position in the space are reported separately throughout. In \cref{fig:sensitivity-analysis}, the space of concerns changes gradually through much of the removal sequence, indicating that the topology is not driven by any single actor. The earliest removals are concentrated among long-established ATS actors and central observer organizations, but the curve steepens only late in the process, when the remaining actor set becomes too thin to sustain the original structure. We therefore interpret the space of concerns as robustly co-produced because, although some actors matter more than others, distributed contributions rather than a lone structural anchor sustain the geometry.

That robustness result should be read together with the temporal mosaic in \cref{fig:decadal-space-stack}. The pooled space of concerns used in the main text is analytically useful, but it remains a long-run composition of historically layered configurations rather than a claim that the latent geometry is literally fixed from decade to decade.

\subsection{Mode Occupancy and Complementarity}
The main text defers the detail of actor position here, because the three-mode partition is no more reproducible than any other cut of this continuum (\cref{sec:partition-bootstrap,sec:class-validation}). \Cref{fig:portfolio_space_ridgelines} places every actor on the concern axis with its dominant mode. Actors are either anchored in one mode or bridge adjacent ones. Restricting attention to strongly anchored sovereign states, pairs drawn from different modes cover more of the agenda between them than size-matched baselines predict, an effect concentrated on the Mode 1--3 boundary; we read this as selective complementarity around a shared core rather than a division of labor, and, like every mode-based statement here, as a description of how actor mass distributes along the ordering rather than evidence that three modes exist.

\subsection{Robustness of the Paired Null Test}\label{sec:paired-null-robustness}
The headline paired test judges each expansion event against the pooled full-history space with a uniform redraw from topics the actor did not hold. Two weaknesses follow. The pooled space uses future information relative to early transitions, and the uniform draw ignores that observed entry concentrates on popular topics, which sit closer to everything in a dense space. We recomputed the same statistic under a $2\times2$ design crossing the space (pooled full-history against cumulative-lagged, built only from documents up to the end of the previous window as in the hazard) with the null (uniform redraw against popularity-weighted redraw, with draw probability proportional to the number of actors specialized in the topic in the previous window, plus one so topics with no prior holders stay available). All four cells use the same 1,055 expansion events and 200 draws per event.

The locality claim survives in every cell. The share of events nearer than their own null is $83.3\%$ under both uniform constructions (reproducing the headline test up to Monte Carlo noise, at $83.0\%$ in the headline script, which consumes a different draw sequence under the same seed), $70.6\%$ under the pooled space with popularity matching, and $66.5\%$ under the strictest combination (cumulative-lagged space, popularity-matched null), where the observed median displacement is $0.588$ against a null median of $0.619$. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test stays below $10^{-15}$ in every cell, and far below it in all but the strictest. Popularity matching matters, and the main text reports the resulting range rather than a single number; none of the constructions approaches chance. \texttt{scripts/paired\_null\_robustness.py} reproduces the four cells.

\subsection{Quasi-experimental probe of the 1991 Madrid Protocol shock}

The locality results are estimated from routine portfolio evolution. A stronger test asks whether pre-shock position also governs the response to an exogenous agenda shock. The 1991 Madrid Protocol is the one such shock in the record, and it arose from the collapse of the minerals regime, the Convention on the Regulation of Antarctic Mineral Resource Activities (CRAMRA) was adopted in Wellington in 1988 and was abandoned after Australia and France declined to ratify it in 1989, and the Protocol negotiation of 1989--91 replaced it \cite{Joyner1998}. This episode is also what produces the pre-1991 jump in \cref{fig:upstream-decisions}, since much of the ``Madrid Protocol set'' in 1986--90 is the CRAMRA controversy itself, so the documentary record anticipates the failed instrument as well as the one that replaced it.

The design uses only pre-1991 inputs where possible. The boosted set is the eight topics whose share of the archive grew most between 1986--90 and 1992--96, restricted to topics with at least $1\%$ pre-presence so that categories created by the Protocol itself cannot enter. Adjacency is the maximum proximity between an actor's pre-1991 portfolio ($RPA \ge 1$) and the boosted set, estimated on 1961--90 documents only. The outcome is the share of an actor's papers in each 5-year window that fall in the boosted set, modeled with window dummies interacted with adjacency, actor and window fixed effects, window-by-pre-breadth controls, and actor-clustered standard errors (30 actors, 258 actor-window observations with at least three papers).

The result is null. Pre-trends are parallel (joint $F(5,\cdot)=0.37$, $p=0.866$), so the design's identifying condition holds, but every post-shock adjacency-by-window coefficient sits near zero (range $-0.85$ to $+0.26$ percentage points, each within 1.5 standard errors of zero). A second-pass entry model asks whether an actor's adjacency to the Protocol's compliance set, relative to the rest of its neighborhood, predicts entry into that set; the coefficient is $-2.66$ (SE $1.83$, $p=0.146$), null to slightly negative. Adjacency is also strongly correlated with pre-breadth ($r=0.84$), which the window-by-breadth controls absorb.

We read the null as a boundary condition on the locality claim rather than a refutation of it. When the regime reoriented collectively, pre-shock position did not discriminate who moved, and the shock was absorbed by the institution as a whole. Locality describes the routine accumulation of portfolios, and exogenous shocks can override it. We report the probe because it is the one design in this paper with genuine identifying assumptions, and its outcome bounds the mechanism reading the main text makes. \texttt{scripts/madrid\_shock\_exploration.py} reproduces the analysis.
