\begin{abstract}
Governing a global commons requires actors with different concerns to assemble a shared agenda. We reconstruct the Antarctic Treaty System's documentary agenda since 1961 as a space of concerns, where concerns lie close when the same actors specialize in both. All concerns belong to one map, but they concentrate locally more than expected from broad actor portfolios and widely held concerns alone. Actors occupy this space unevenly and add new specializations near earlier portfolios. In retrospective tests, papers about target and nearby concerns improve predictions of the focus of non-binding Resolutions but provide no clear gain for Decisions or potentially binding Measures. Measures instead concentrate in recurring area-management work and most cite earlier formal instruments. The concern space links distributed attention to agenda structure and separates institutional change from continuity in potentially binding output.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} agenda formation, consensus governance, global commons, environmental governance, space of concerns, relational analysis, Antarctic Treaty

\noindent\textbf{Science for Society}
\newline
Governments face more environmental concerns than they can address at once. Participants distribute effort and expertise across a shared agenda, while adopted texts show only the settled end of this process. Using six decades of Antarctic Treaty papers, we map concerns by the actors who specialize in them. Broad participation connects the agenda, but local concentrations remain and actors occupy them differently. New specializations occur near earlier portfolios. Retrospective tests show that paper attention helps identify the categories addressed by non-binding Resolutions, including when the predictors use only the preceding meeting. They show no clear gain for Decisions or potentially binding Measures. Other institutions with repeated actors, stable categories, public inputs, and observable outputs could use this approach to follow how collective attention organizes an agenda. Legal force, implementation, and environmental outcomes require separate evidence.

\section{Introduction}
Collective governance turns differing priorities into shared decisions. In global commons, state and institutional actors must reconcile their distinct concerns with responsibility for a shared environment \cite{BurchEtAl2019}. Submitted documents make part of this process visible by framing problems and proposing responses. Adopted texts preserve the result of agreement but omit the preceding distribution of attention \cite{BachrachBaratz1962,Stone2013}. Formal agreement can also leave contestation unresolved \cite{Drubel2023HiddenContestation}. These texts then enter an institutional record in which later attention and action unfold. We study this recurring process in the Antarctic Treaty system, using documentary attention before formal adoption to reconstruct the shared agenda, follow how actor portfolios develop within it, and test whether attention anticipates the concerns addressed in adopted texts.

Governance agendas change as issues gain and lose attention, often through long periods of stability interrupted by sharper shifts \cite{BaumgartnerJones1993}. Policymakers allocate limited attention as they process information and prioritize problems \cite{JonesBaumgartner2005}. Comparative research traces these dynamics across political systems \cite{GreenPedersenWalgrave2014}. Institutional performance varies across tasks and policy domains, and process indicators capture different dimensions from outcome indicators \cite{GutnerThompson2010,CoenEtAl2022}. These processes unfold within a wider system of treaties and organizations. Citation ties reveal an evolving set of linked multilateral environmental agreements \cite{Kim2013}. At the system level, these agreements can combine fragmentation, polycentricity, and complexity \cite{Kim2020}. Within this system, actors also connect concerns through their work. Each actor approaches new work from an existing portfolio, and overlapping portfolios place some concerns nearer than others. This map lets us test whether actors add new specializations near their earlier work. Relating concerns through shared actors connects the agenda's overall organization to the paths actors take through it.

Actors connect concerns through the work they submit. We call an actor specialized in a concern when that concern makes up a larger share of its documents than it does across the archive. The concerns in which an actor specializes form its portfolio. When the same actors specialize in two concerns, we place those concerns close together in a \emph{space of concerns}. We then test whether actors add new specializations near their earlier portfolios.

Following this process requires records of the same actors across multiple meetings, documents submitted before formal adoption, concern categories that remain comparable over time, and outputs distinguished by legal form. Consensus institutions make such records especially useful. Adoption shows that participants accepted a text, but the absence of roll calls leaves the strength of their support unobserved. Participants may also reach agreement by narrowing or weakening a text's requirements \cite{Steinberg2002,Drubel2023HiddenContestation}. Outputs differ in legal force \cite{AbbottSnidal2000}. We therefore compare how documentary attention relates to each form of output.

The Antarctic Treaty System (ATS) preserves this record across six decades. The 1959 Treaty established peace and scientific cooperation, while later agendas cover climate change, growing tourism, conservation demands, and geopolitical pressures \cite{Carter2025,Luo2024AntarcticTourism,Dodds2019,AntarcticTreaty1959}. Its consultative meetings have governed activity across Antarctica by consensus since 1961 \cite{Joyner1998,Yao2021}. Participants submit papers that raise problems, present evidence, and propose action before meetings adopt formal texts \cite{Kennicutt2019,Sampaio2022}. The archive applies a consistent retrospective concern taxonomy to those papers. Since 1995, the adopted record distinguishes non-binding Resolutions, organizational Decisions, and Measures that can become legally binding after the required approvals. Together, these records let us follow documentary attention into outputs with different legal consequences.

We analyse 6,573 working papers and information documents that 66 state and institutional actors submitted between 1961 and 2025. Preparing a paper requires time and resources, so these documents show where participants direct documentary effort. They cannot tell whether that effort reflects strategy, mandate, expertise, or reporting duties. We use the documents in two analyses. First, we map shared specialization and test whether actors add specializations near their earlier portfolios. The resulting distances measure shared specialization; semantic similarity, collaboration, and causal pathways require separate evidence. Second, at each meeting we fit models on earlier meetings and compare the categories addressed by earlier outputs with predictions from models that add papers about the target and nearby concerns. We predict the concern categories that Measures, Decisions, and Resolutions address. This design adapts relatedness measures used to study movement among products, industries, technologies, and research fields \cite{Hidalgo2007,Hidalgo2018,Neffke2011,Boschma2017,Guevara2016}.

The ATS agenda forms one connected concern space in which actors sit in particular niches. Broad actor portfolios and widely held concerns account for its connectivity. Local concentrations remain after we hold both features fixed. Actors occupy this common space unevenly, and new specializations occur more often near their earlier portfolios. Papers about a concern and its neighbours improve predictions of the concerns addressed by Resolutions in 15 of 19 meetings. A sensitivity analysis using only papers from the preceding meeting also improves the mean score. Papers provide no clear improvement for Decisions or Measures. Measures instead concentrate in recurring area-management work, and most cite earlier formal instruments. This evidence identifies which concerns appear in adopted texts, not their legal force, implementation, or environmental outcomes. Within that boundary, documentary attention anticipates the concerns addressed in non-binding Resolutions, while potentially binding Measures repeat recurring work and cite earlier formal instruments.

\section{Results}
The Antarctic Treaty System (ATS) governs activity across Antarctica through an agenda that extends far beyond the Treaty's founding articles \cite{Joyner1998,AntarcticTreaty1959}. We reconstruct that agenda from 6,573 papers submitted by 66 state and institutional actors between 1961 and 2025. Each of the 45 circles represents a concern in the Secretariat's paper taxonomy. Two concerns lie close when the same actors specialize in both. We measure this relation with the lower of the two conditional probabilities, $\phi$ (\cref{eq:rpa,eq:proximity}). The map records shared specialization. It does not measure semantic similarity, collaboration, or causal influence.

All 45 concerns belong to one connected map with distinct local concentrations. Only 39 of 990 concern pairs have a shared-specialization score of zero, so every concern connects directly or indirectly to every other concern (\cref{fig:the_space_of_concerns}b). Shuffled maps that preserve each actor's number of specializations and each concern's number of specialized actors also connect every concern and reproduce most of the overall prevalence of positive relations. They do not reproduce how strongly relations concentrate within the displayed regions. Weighted modularity, which summarizes that concentration, is $Q=0.090$ in the observed map and $0.052$ at the null median (95\% interval $[0.044,0.063]$, $p=0.001$; \cref{sec:fixed-margin-null}).

The displayed regions in \cref{fig:the_space_of_concerns}a show local differentiation within this connected map. Procedural and scientific work forms a broad core. Environmental management, tourism and safety, and resources and planning form denser concentrations around it. Specialized actor sets overlap across their boundaries. Inspections, area protection, and environmental protection bridge the displayed regions. Measures of overall reach and cross-region bridging distinguish widely connected concerns from bridges between regions (\cref{fig:the_space_of_concerns}c,d), while paper volume follows a different hierarchy. Counts identify where attention accumulates. Shared specialization identifies how concerns share actor portfolios. Checks on labels, the range of each actor's specializations, and actor removal leave this distinction intact (\cref{sec:counts-versus-relations,sec:substantive-sensitivity,sec:space-robustness}).

\subsection{Actors develop portfolios locally}
Although the map connects all concerns, actors do not hold the same portfolios. Australia spans procedural, environmental, resource, cooperation, and reporting work, whereas the International Association of Antarctica Tour Operators concentrates on access, tourism, and safety. Russia centers on the procedural core with extensions into resources and environmental concerns (\cref{fig:local-specialization}a--d). Later entrants also begin from less connected positions: actors joining in 1991--2010 specialize in concerns whose full-record holders cover fewer concerns and which have fewer nearby concerns than the career-stage benchmark (\cref{sec:cohort-method,sec:secondary-structure-results,tab:cohort-ratios}). This retrospective comparison does not identify why those entry positions differ.

Across actors, portfolios develop locally. New specializations occur beside concerns an actor already holds, as Australia illustrates across successive five-meeting windows (\cref{fig:local-specialization}e--g). Across the archive, the rate falls from $9.4\%$ in the nearest distance decile to $2.7\%$ in the farthest. After accounting for prior popularity, each $0.1$ increase in distance corresponds to $18.3\%$ lower odds of specialization (OR $0.817$, 95\% interval $[0.783,0.853]$; \cref{fig:local-specialization}h). Alternative windows, map constructions, and entry definitions preserve this direction (\cref{sec:substantive-sensitivity,sec:paired-null-robustness,tab:locality_estimands}). The result describes a pattern of portfolio development, not its cause.

\subsection{Attention predicts soft-law decisions}
Documentary attention helps locate the concerns addressed by non-binding Resolutions. We align the paper taxonomy with the 15 official output categories and compare the categories addressed by earlier outputs with papers about the target and nearby categories (\cref{sec:outcome-positioning,sec:prospective-output-forecast}). Across 19 retrospective predictions evaluated in sequence, papers about the target and nearby concerns improve the Resolution prediction in 15 meetings. Relative to the categories addressed by earlier Resolutions, the combined model lowers the mean prediction score by $0.065$ (95\% meeting-bootstrap interval $[-0.114,-0.014]$; \cref{fig:selective-translation}a). It provides no clear gain for Measures or Decisions (\cref{sec:forecast-results,tab:output-forecast-scores}).

Paper volume does not explain the improvement; the observed structure of the concern space does. That is, it outperforms every label-shuffled map in the Resolution analysis, and the combined result remains negative in a preceding-meeting sensitivity analysis (\cref{fig:selective-translation}c; \cref{sec:forecast-audits}). Resolutions also span a broader set of categories than Decisions or Measures, leaving more variation for current attention to locate. These tests support a retrospective association between attention and soft-law focus. They do not show that papers cause adoption or that the full predictor set was available in real time.

The paper-to-output alignment requires an author-coded bridge from 45 paper concerns to 15 official output categories. Sensitivity analyses retain the overall attention association, but reduce confidence in a separate contribution from papers about nearby concerns (\cref{sec:forecast-audits}). We therefore treat the combined Resolution result as the central finding.

Restricting all three analyses to papers attributed to Consultative Parties at each meeting leaves all 45 concerns connected and preserves local portfolio development (OR $0.900$, 95\% interval $[0.860,0.942]$). Under the same restriction, target and nearby attention still improves Resolution prediction (mean score change $-0.050$, 95\% meeting-bootstrap interval $[-0.100,-0.003]$), while the Decision and Measure intervals include zero (\cref{sec:consultative-party-sensitivity,fig:consultative-party-sensitivity}).

\subsection{Measures continue recurring formal work}
Potentially binding Measures instead concentrate in recurring area-management work. Of 277 Measures, 266 carry that category, much of it involving Antarctic Specially Protected Area (ASPA) designations and management-plan revisions. ASPAs are areas designated under Annex V of the Environmental Protocol to protect outstanding environmental, scientific, historic, aesthetic, or wilderness values, or ongoing or planned scientific research \cite{ATSProtectedAreas}. Measure titles explicitly name an ASPA in 160 cases, a management plan in 230, and a revised management plan in 211 (\cref{fig:measure-composition}). This concentration leaves little topical variation for prediction and may favour a baseline based on earlier Measures. That baseline outperforms models based on earlier Decisions, Resolutions, or paper attention alone, and adding current attention provides no clear improvement (\cref{sec:forecast-results}).

Measures also retain a formal record of that continuity. In total, 245 cite at least one earlier instrument, most often another Measure (\cref{fig:selective-translation}b). Exact citations give a conservative account because final Measures can omit intermediate steps. They identify formal reference, not a general causal path from papers to law or entry into force (\cref{sec:formal-inheritance-results,tab:measure-formal-inheritance}).

\section{Discussion}
Understanding collective governance requires tracing complex institutions, their internal and external relations, and the rules that organize decision-making. Governance is often observed through the decisions it produces. We instead make the preceding process tractable. We construct a space of concerns that represents how participants in the Antarctic Treaty System distribute documentary attention collectively and how individual actors occupy that shared space.

The concern space is connected but locally organized. Actors occupy it selectively and tend to add new specializations near concerns already in their portfolios. Actors with broader portfolios also tend to remain active for longer. Documentary attention predicts the category focus of non-binding Resolutions, whereas it provides no clear improvement for organizational Decisions or potentially binding Measures. For those outputs, earlier adopted texts remain more informative (\cref{fig:selective-translation}).

These results connect patterns that previous research usually studies separately. Work on bounded attention shows that institutions concentrate on selected problems and shift attention unevenly among them \cite{BaumgartnerJones1993,JonesBaumgartner2005,GreenPedersenWalgrave2014}. Research on relatedness finds that actors tend to enter activities near their existing capabilities, producing path-dependent development across products, industries, technologies, and research fields \cite{Hidalgo2007,Neffke2011,Boschma2017,Guevara2016}. Scholarship on soft law distinguishes flexible, non-binding responses from instruments that impose stronger legal obligations \cite{AbbottSnidal2000}. We observe these processes within one documentary record. The concern space links individual portfolio development to the organization of the collective agenda, while the prediction analysis connects their combined documentary attention to the categories addressed in adopted texts. This joint view shows how actors' local trajectories contribute to collective decision-making without treating either individual behavior or aggregate attention as sufficient on its own.

Resolutions and Measures preserve different parts of the governance process. Resolutions retain information about shifts in documentary attention, whereas Measures retain stronger continuity with recurring formal work. The dominance of recurring protected-area designations and management-plan revisions means that this contrast may reflect output composition as well as legal form. The concern space makes these patterns comparable by linking the collective organization of attention to the trajectories of individual actors. For environmental governance, this reveals where new concerns are gathering institutional attention and where earlier commitments continue to structure formal action.

Our approach observes documentary effort rather than the preferences, bargaining, influence, implementation, or environmental change that also shape governance. While documentary effort shows some of this implicitly, power can operate through agenda exclusion \cite{BachrachBaratz1962}, unwritten rules and informal influence can modify formal treaty provisions \cite{Stone2013}, and apparent acceptance can coexist with hidden contestation \cite{Drubel2023HiddenContestation}. Institutional outputs also remain distinct from implementation and problem-solving outcomes \cite{GutnerThompson2010,CoenEtAl2022}. The concern space represents shared actor portfolios rather than semantic similarity or collaboration. The paper--output comparison depends on an author-coded alignment; a blinded human audit agreed with 38 of its 45 assignments, while the remaining disagreements identify boundary cases.

The method turns repeated institutional records into a joint account of individual trajectories, collective attention, and formal output. Formal texts record what institutions agree to address; the space of concerns reveals the attention that precedes agreement.

\section{Data and Methods}
\subsection{Data and archive coverage}
We analyse working papers and information documents submitted from 1961 to 2025. ``ATS'' refers to the broader governance system anchored in the Antarctic Treaty, but our corpus comes only from the Treaty-centred archive of Antarctic Treaty Consultative Meetings (ATCMs). The Secretariat's taxonomy also includes topics handled by the Committee for Environmental Protection because these matters reach the ATCM agenda. Meeting volume rises partly for institutional reasons: ATCMs became annual in the 1990s, and the Committee has normally met annually since 1998. Conference Room Papers lack systematic availability, so we exclude them. The corpus therefore observes formal documentary preparation, not the informal negotiation record.

The official archive supplies thematic search categories, submitters, and document types \cite{Yaryab2024}. We retain Secretariat papers, so the Secretariat appears both as maintainer of the database categories and as one of the 66 submitting actors. Its papers enter on the same terms as those of other submitters.

We collected the data on 6 February 2026 from the ATS website (\url{https://www.ats.aq/devAS/Meetings/DocDatabase?lang=e}) with Carlo Hamalainen's antarctic-database-go collector (\url{https://github.com/carlohamalainen/antarctic-database-go}). The collector stores HTTP responses, source documents, and processing status in SQLite. These cached records permit deterministic reruns even if the website changes.

We do not derive the categories from paper text. They are the harmonized cross-meeting categories under which the Secretariat-maintained database returns each paper. A paper can appear under several categories, although the returned paper payload does not itself carry a category field. The query responses also record no category-assignment date. The concern space therefore describes shared specialization within an institutional taxonomy, not semantic similarity. We will include the processed many-to-many panel and reconstruction code in the next Zenodo version.

We reconstructed paper--category relations from the collector's cached category-query responses, then deduplicated attachment-linked records by \texttt{paper\_id}. This produced 6,591 unique papers. We removed 18 papers (0.3\%, all after 2010) returned only under the placeholders \texttt{ALL} or \texttt{Other}, leaving 6,573 papers and 7,895 substantive paper--category relations. Of the retained papers, 5,345 have one category and 1,228 have two to five. We divide a paper with $k$ categories equally among them, so it contributes $1/k$ to each label. Papers with multiple sponsors are then expanded to one row per submitting actor; each sponsor receives the paper's same fractional category weights. The extracted and reconstructed dataset contains the following columns.

A separate snapshot from 29 January 2024 contains the same 45 categories. All 6,230 matched papers with a nonmissing snapshot category retain that category in the 2026 reconstruction. The snapshot stores only one category per paper, does not date that assignment, and does not cover ATCMs 46--47. It supports label stability but cannot establish when every multi-label membership became available.

The unit is the \emph{submitting actor}, not legal status under the Treaty. We include Consultative Parties, Non-Consultative Parties, Observers, invited Expert organizations, and the Secretariat because all appear as submitters in this documentary arena. Only Consultative Parties take decisions. The actor types are therefore not institutionally equivalent, and aggregated estimates describe documentary activity rather than formal authority \cite{Sampaio2022}. Sixty-six actors appear in the corpus. The extracted field \texttt{party\_type} contains Working Paper and Information Paper codes, not Treaty status. Actor-role information is separate and can change over time. The cohort comparison retains 43 actors, while the states-only locality check retains 42 states. Conference Room Papers do not enter any analysis.

\subsection{Building the concern map}\label{sec:map-construction}
Counts and shared specialization answer different questions. Counts show where attention is common. Shared specialization shows which concerns receive a disproportionate share of the same actors' papers. A concern pursued by nearly everyone can be prominent in counts but uninformative for shared specialization because no actor stands out. A small technical group can produce the opposite pattern. Our map records which concerns share specialized actors, not the overall importance of a concern.

Let $x(a,i)$ denote the fractionally counted submissions by actor $a$ on concern $i$. If the archive returns paper $p$ under $k_p$ substantive categories, it contributes $1/k_p$ to each; a co-sponsored paper contributes those weights to every listed submitting actor. Actors include Consultative Parties, Non-Consultative Parties, Observers, invited Expert organizations, and the ATS Secretariat whenever they appear as submitters. We define the normalised activity share
\begin{equation}\label{eq:activity_share}
\pi(a,i) = \frac{x(a,i)}{\sum_j x(a,j)},
\end{equation}

and the system-wide importance of topic $i$ as

\begin{equation}\label{eq:topic_importance}
Z_i = \frac{\sum_c x(c,i)}{\sum_{d,j} x(d,j)}.
\end{equation}

The Revealed Policy Advantage (RPA) index is then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rpa}
 \rho(a,i) = \frac{\pi(a,i)}{Z_i}.
\end{equation}
We call this ratio \emph{revealed policy advantage} rather than the usual \emph{revealed comparative advantage} (RCA) because the units are policy concerns rather than exported products. The mathematics does not change, but the interpretation is narrower. Submissions have not passed a competitive market test. Actors may submit to inform, comply, signal, or seek action. RPA records the concerns receiving a disproportionate share of an actor's papers. It cannot separate intent from mandate, expertise, reporting duties, or document-production capacity, and it cannot observe priorities that produce no paper. Scientific capacity may also enter the measure because Article IX(2) ties consultative status to the scale of scientific research activity. Nor do concerns have an equivalent of product complexity or economic value. Shared specialization remains useful because it records how actors allocate finite documentary effort across the agenda.

We measure how strongly each pair of concerns shares specialized actors. We call this value proximity:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:proximity}
\phi(i,j)
=
\min\!\left\{
\begin{aligned}
 &\Pr\!\bigl(\rho(a,i) \ge 1 \mid \rho(a,j) \ge 1\bigr),\\
 &\Pr\!\bigl(\rho(a,j) \ge 1 \mid \rho(a,i) \ge 1\bigr)
\end{aligned}
\right\}.
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
 \Pr(\rho(a,j)\ge1 \mid \rho(a,i)\ge1) =\frac{\sum_a \mathbf{1}[\rho(a,i)\ge1 \land \rho(a,j)\ge1]}
 {\sum_a \mathbf{1}[\rho(a,i)\ge1]},
\end{equation}
This produces a weighted map of relations among concerns. Conditional probability allows two uncommon concerns to be close when specialization in one reliably accompanies specialization in the other; a joint probability would shrink mechanically with rarity. Taking the lower of the two directions prevents a niche concern from appearing close to a broad concern merely because the niche usually occurs alongside it. The resulting score is symmetric. We estimate it across actors in the relevant window. With 66 actors, individual relations rest on modest counts: the strongest, between Marine living resources and Mineral resources, rests on 6 actors. The map is therefore a coarse aggregate rather than a high-resolution estimate of every pair. We construct the full-record map and rebuild time-specific maps within each window. This adapts product-space proximity to governance concerns \cite{Hidalgo2007}.

\subsection{Can marginal counts reconstruct the concern space?}\label{sec:counts-versus-relations}
To test whether proximity is only a reformulation of prevalence, we treat each of the 990 concern pairs as one observation and use full-record proximity $\phi(i,j)$ as the outcome. For each pair, the paper-count model contains the sum, absolute difference, and minimum of the two concerns' log-transformed marginal paper volumes. The actor-prevalence model uses the corresponding functions of the number of actors with $\mathrm{RPA}\ge1$. A combined model includes both sets. These are reconstruction diagnostics, not causal models.

We assess generalization with a leave-endpoint-out procedure. For each concern, we omit every pair containing it, fit the model to the remaining pairs, and predict the omitted relations. We predict each pair twice, using one fit that excludes each endpoint, and average the two predictions before calculating $R^2$. We then resample the 66 complete actor histories 1,000 times and rebuild paper volume, RPA assignments, the full shared-specialization matrix, and every model in each draw. Finally, 9,999 quadratic assignment procedure (QAP) label permutations randomly reassign the observed marginal attributes to concerns while leaving the matrix fixed. This preserves the marginal distributions while testing whether their observed alignment with the concern map exceeds arbitrary assignments.

Paper volume explains $12.8\%$ of pairwise shared-specialization variation in the pooled fit and $10.7\%$ when predicting relations involving a concern omitted from model fitting (\cref{fig:the-space-of-concerns-reconstruction-appendix,tab:count-reconstruction}). Specialization prevalence explains $35.3\%$ under that fit and $34.1\%$ under the same test. The combined model reaches $35.9\%$ across all pairs but remains at $34.1\%$ when the procedure omits concerns. Across actor bootstraps, the additional all-pairs variance explained by paper volume after actor prevalence has a median of $0.005$ and a central 95\% interval of $[0.0003,0.0173]$. All three observed pooled $R^2$ values exceed every corresponding QAP draw ($p=0.0001$). Marginal prevalence therefore aligns with the concern space, as expected for a score constructed from specialization, but it does not reconstruct most pairwise variation. \path{scripts/counts_vs_relations.py} reproduces the analysis.

\subsection{Testing whether specialization shifts toward nearby concerns}\label{sec:hazard-model}
We compare adjacent rolling five-meeting windows. A concern enters the comparison only when the actor was not already specialized in it. For actor $a$, concern $i$, and transition from $t-1$ to $t$, this condition is
\[
\text{at-risk}_{a i t}=1 \iff \rho_{a i, t-1} < 1.
\]
The binary outcome is then
\[
y_{a i t}=1 \iff \rho_{a i, t} \ge 1 \ \text{and}\ \rho_{a i, t-1} < 1.
\]
Let the previously active support be
\[
S_{a,t-1}=\{j:\rho_{aj,t-1}\ge 1\},
\]
let $A_{t-1}$ be the concerns that have appeared in the archive before the transition, and let the available set be
\[
R_{at}=\{i\in A_{t-1}:\rho_{ai,t-1}<1\},
\]
and let $m_{at}=\sum_{i\in R_{at}} y_{ait}$ denote the number of specialization crossings in an actor-period. Our main locality specification is then an actor-period conditional logit,
\begin{dmath}
\Pr(\mathbf{y}_{at}\mid m_{at}, X_{at})
\propto
\exp\!\left(\sum_{i\in R_{at}}y_{ait}\bigl(\beta_d d_{a i, t-1}\!+\! \beta_p \mathrm{pop}_{i,t-1}
\bigr)
\right),
\end{dmath}
where
\[
d_{a i, t-1}=1-\max_{j\in S_{a,t-1}}\phi(i,j)
\]
is the distance from candidate concern $i$ to the actor's earlier portfolio and $\mathrm{pop}_{i,t-1}$ is its prior popularity (the share of actors with $\mathrm{RPA} \ge 1$). The model holds constant how many concerns cross the threshold in that period. It therefore compares which available concerns cross for the same actor at the same time, rather than comparing actors with different general rates of expansion. Periods in which an actor has no earlier portfolio or no threshold crossing do not enter the model. Differences in the number of concerns already held are constant within each actor-period comparison. The map, portfolio membership, and crossings all use $\mathrm{RPA}\ge1$.

The prospective rule leaves 968 actor-period comparisons and 30,957 actor-period--concern options. The distance coefficient is $-2.02$ (standard error [SE] $0.22$), or an odds ratio of $0.817$ per $+0.1$ distance (95\% model-based confidence interval [CI] $[0.783,0.853]$). The rule is primary because neither the map nor the choice set uses information first observed during the transition. A second outcome requires no paper on the concern in the earlier window and at least one in the next. It gives an odds ratio of $0.874$ ($[0.834,0.916]$), showing that the local association is not only a consequence of the relative specialization threshold. \path{scripts/compare_major_results_category_treatments.py} reproduces both estimates.

We construct the example trajectories in Supplementary \cref{fig:actor-continuous-trajectories} on a continuous axis. We compute RPA in rolling five-meeting windows and locate each actor on the horizontal axis of the fixed full-record map as the RPA-weighted mean of its concern coordinates. We omit windows with fewer than three concerns at $\mathrm{RPA}\ge1$. Australia connects this continuous summary to the snapshots in \cref{fig:local-specialization}, while the Netherlands and Ukraine provide two further examples. The trajectories illustrate movement rather than testing locality. \texttt{figS22\_actor\_trajectories.py} reproduces the figure.

Because the rolling windows overlap, successive choice sets for the same actor are not statistically independent. We therefore resample complete actor histories and rebuild portfolios, historical availability, the cumulative map, and the choice model over 500 bootstrap replicates. We also use disjoint five-meeting blocks and restrict the panel to states. The blocks reduce rolling-window overlap, but successive actor transitions still share their middle block and use nested cumulative maps; the actor-history bootstrap addresses this dependence. The association remains negative in both checks; with disjoint blocks, the odds ratio is $0.904$ ($[0.855,0.957]$). We report these checks in \cref{tab:locality_estimands}. The result also remains stable across the full-record, previous-window, and earlier-record maps while holding the prospective choice set fixed (\cref{fig:hazard-space-sensitivity}). The primary distance uses raw $1-\phi$, which has bounds and a direct interpretation; $-\log\phi$ is unstable when two concerns have zero proximity. \path{scripts/bootstrap_fractional_locality.py} and \path{scripts/hazard_robustness_dependence.py} reproduce the dependence checks.

We right-censor spells still active at the end of 2025 and compute a Kaplan--Meier curve for each gap definition. The allowed gap delays any failure declaration, so every curve mechanically equals one over that initial interval. \Cref{fig:retention-curves} reports all five definitions on the unshifted time-since-entry scale. The analysis does not distinguish a concern-specific lapse from years in which the actor submits nothing, so it is a descriptive sensitivity analysis rather than evidence of a retention mechanism.

\subsection{Checks on local specialization}\label{sec:substantive-sensitivity}
This subsection tests whether local specialization depends on the temporal construction of the space, the threshold-crossing definition, self-contribution to the shared-specialization matrix, or full rather than fractional credit for co-sponsored papers. A separate subsection below asks whether a small number of actors dominates the recovered space.

We first change how we build the concern map while keeping the prospective choice set fixed. \Cref{fig:hazard-space-sensitivity} compares the full-record, previous-window, and earlier-record maps. In each case, distance is $d=1-\max\phi$. Moving $0.1$ farther gives odds ratios of $0.67$, $0.78$, and $0.82$, respectively. Each model adjusts for prior concern popularity when estimating the distance association. The earlier-record map is primary because it uses no later shared-specialization information. We retain raw $1-\phi$ because its values lie between zero and one; taking its logarithm is unstable when two concerns have a score of zero.

\subsection{Reading the map at two levels of detail}\label{sec:cpm-reading}
We use the Constant Potts Model (CPM) to group concerns with relatively strong shared-specialization scores \cite{Traag2019}. CPM receives $\phi$ directly as the strength of a relation, not $1-\phi$ as a distance. The setting $\gamma$ controls the level of detail. CPM tends to place two concerns in the same region when their score exceeds $\gamma$ and separate them when it falls below $\gamma$. Varying $\gamma$ therefore gives progressively finer readings rather than one uniquely correct grouping.

The four-region reading at $\gamma=0.211$ contains groups of 30, 7, 5, and 3 concerns. The seven-region reading at $\gamma=0.273$ contains groups of 16, 13, 6, 4, 2, 2, and 2. The finer reading is mostly nested within the coarser one: 43 of 45 concerns fall within a single coarse parent, giving fine-to-coarse purity of $0.956$. Two standard measures of agreement between groupings give an adjusted Rand index of $0.478$ and normalised mutual information of $0.707$. The exceptions are Cooperation with Other Organisations and ATS reports, which form one fine pair across two coarse regions, and Marine Acoustics and Sub-glacial Lakes, which form another. \Cref{fig:cpm-partition-comparison} places both groupings on identical coordinates. \path{fig_cpm_partition_comparison.py} reproduces the comparison.

\subsection{Do actor breadth and concern prevalence explain the map?}\label{sec:fixed-margin-null}
We test whether the number of concerns held by each actor and the number of actors specializing in each concern explain the map. The shuffled comparison begins with the binary actor--concern matrix at $\mathrm{RPA}\ge1$. Curveball trades randomize which actors hold which concerns while preserving both sets of totals. The seven displayed CPM regions come from the observed map, so holding their labels fixed in shuffled draws gives supporting evidence rather than an independent test. \subsection{Aligning papers and adopted texts by category}\label{sec:outcome-positioning}
The output inventory contains all 584 records with titles from regular ATCMs 19--47 (1995--2025): 277 Measures, 135 Decisions, and 172 Resolutions. We begin in 1995 because Decision 1 (1995) established these output types and the official register is complete for the period. Measures become legally binding after the required approvals. Decisions address internal organizational matters and are operative on adoption or another stated date. Resolutions are hortatory and not legally binding. We therefore group them only as \emph{adopted ATCM outputs}; the count does not imply equal legal effect. Instruments adopted at Special ATCMs, including the 1991 Madrid Protocol, are outside this inventory. Each record retains instrument type, number, year, and meeting because numbers repeat across years. The analysis reads these records directly from the archived 9 February 2026 official export and verifies its SHA-256 checksum, total counts, type counts, and counts by meeting.

Adopted texts do not enter the 45-concern paper map as additional concerns. The official instrument register instead assigns every retained instrument to one or more of 15 broad categories. We place each paper concern within one of these instrument families and rebuild a separate paper-derived map at the 15-category resolution. This alignment compares paper attention with the category distribution of adopted instruments without treating an instrument as a point in the concern space. It uses shared categories, not citations from instruments to papers. For a paper returned under several fine concerns, we sum its fractional weights within families; they still total one. Sixty instruments have more than one official category, giving 646 instrument--category relations; an instrument with $k$ categories contributes $1/k$ to each. The resulting weights also total one for every instrument.

This 45-to-15 hierarchy is the assignment the Antarctic Secretariate makes and is available through their online portal. It groups concerns by the scope of the official instrument categories. For example, Management Plans, Environmental Domains Analysis, Marine Protected Areas, and the general area-protection concern all enter ``Area protection and management''. \Cref{tab:output-category-crosswalk} shows the complete hierarchy. \path{scripts/official_regular_atcm_outputs.py} freezes the mapping, and \path{scripts/analyze_output_category_families.py} rebuilds the broad paper map and instrument analysis.

We audited the hierarchy with one human coder blinded to the analytical assignments and forecast results. The agent received only the 45 concern labels, the 15 official output families in randomized order, and an instruction to select each concern's principal institutional function. The coder matched 38 of 45 analytical assignments (84.4\%). The coder recorded high confidence for 28 assignments, medium confidence for 15, and low confidence for two. The seven disagreements involved CEP Strategy Discussions, Marine Acoustics, Drilling, Operation of the Antarctic Treaty system Reports, Biological Prospecting, Inspections, and Operation of the CEP. We retain the analytical hierarchy and treat these labels as boundary cases in the mapping-sensitivity analysis. \path{tools/build_crosswalk_blind_validation.py} creates the blinded packet, and \path{output/scientific_checks/crosswalk_blind_coder_a_comparison.csv} records the item-level audit.

We test dependence on this hierarchy using 27 boundary concerns and 50 plausible alternative assignments based on the concern labels. Each one-at-a-time test rebuilds paper-family relations, historical maps, papers about the target category, and papers weighted by nearby categories. We also evaluate 48 sparse joint mappings, in which each boundary concern has a 20\% reassignment probability, and 48 broad joint mappings drawn uniformly from the retained options. These ensembles generate stress scenarios rather than probabilities for the correct mapping. A post hoc failure search combines the individually most adverse assignments. Because it selects assignments using evaluation scores, it locates vulnerability rather than expected performance under coding error. Official instrument categories, evaluation meetings, model settings, and scoring remain fixed. \path{scripts/check_crosswalk_uncertainty.py} reproduces these checks.

\subsection{Retrospective predictions of adopted text categories}\label{sec:prospective-output-forecast}
We test whether papers help locate the categories of adopted texts. The target is not the total number of instruments. For each form of output and each evaluation meeting in sequence, it is the distribution of that meeting's instrument weight across the 15 official categories. If instrument $r$ at meeting $t$ assigns observed share $q_{irt}$ to category $i$, and a model assigns predicted share $\widehat{p}_{irt}$, we score the prediction as
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal{L}_{rt}=-\sum_i q_{irt}\log \widehat{p}_{irt}.
\end{equation}
Lower values mean that the model placed more probability on the categories where the meeting adopted output.

We select the specification using Measures, Decisions, and Resolutions together from ATCMs 25--28. Candidate settings include windows of 3, 5, 8, or 10 meetings of earlier outputs and windows of 1, 2, 3, or 5 meetings of papers about the target concern. We also compare regularization values of $0.1$, $0.2$, $0.5$, and $1.0$. This procedure selects five meetings of earlier outputs, one meeting of earlier papers about the target concern, and regularization $0.1$. We freeze those settings and run a sequence of predictions trained on earlier meetings for ATCMs 29--47, giving 19 evaluation meetings for each form of output. At each evaluation meeting, we fit the model on ATCM 20 through the meeting immediately before it. A regularized Poisson model includes category indicators and standardised numerical predictors. We normalise predictions across categories before scoring. Earlier exploratory analyses had already examined ATCMs 29--47, so this period provides a retrospective test trained only on earlier meetings instead of external validation.

The benchmark contains the preceding five meetings of the same form of output in the target category. The target-category paper model adds fractionally counted papers and the number of distinct submitting actors in that category during the immediately preceding meeting, plus the same two quantities for the target meeting. Participants tabled the paper files before meeting outputs, but the category memberships used to aggregate them come from 2026 query responses. Because those responses lack assignment timestamps, we treat the exercise as a retrospective same-meeting prediction. The full model adds papers from the target meeting in every non-target category with a positive relation, up to 14. For each target category, we normalise shared-specialization scores from the earlier-paper map to one and apply them to paper counts from the target meeting. This addition uses every positively related category and therefore requires no chosen number of neighbours. We rebuild the broad map from papers submitted before the target meeting.

We compare models within each evaluation meeting and resample the 19 paired score differences 20,000 times to form 95\% intervals. Contrasts among forms of output resample the paired difference between Resolution gains and the corresponding Measure or Decision gains. Robustness checks vary the earlier-output window, target-paper window, regularization, and number of neighbours. They also omit each target category from scoring, replace papers about nearby concerns with all other papers, and project current papers through the original 45-concern map. Further checks split the evaluation period and use moving-block meeting bootstraps. A separate check reselects every setting using only meetings before each evaluation meeting.

As a post hoc concentration check, we calculate the Shannon entropy of the adopted Resolution distribution within each evaluation meeting and split the 19 meetings at the median. We report the mean score change in each half, their difference, and the correlation between score change and entropy. Intervals resample meetings within each half. This check asks whether the gain appears only in meetings with unusually broad Resolution output; it does not make Measures, Decisions, and Resolutions compositionally equivalent. \path{scripts/analyze_resolution_concentration_sensitivity.py} reproduces the calculation.

The shuffled-map comparison uses 200 label permutations. At each meeting, it applies the same permutation to the rows and columns of the matrix built from earlier papers, recomputes the paper counts weighted by nearby concerns, and refits every prediction. The test therefore breaks which categories occupy neighbouring positions while preserving a coherent symmetric map. Because lower score changes are better, its lower-tail probability is $(1+b)/(1+200)$, where $b$ counts shuffled changes less than or equal to the observed change. The Area protection and management check omits that target row from scoring but leaves its papers available as attention to nearby categories. \path{scripts/analyze_resolution_attention_forecast.py} reproduces the primary predictions and checks; \path{scripts/evaluate_nested_attention_forecast.py} reproduces the within-fold selection sensitivity.

Three post hoc checks probe the use of information unavailable at the time and the persistence of attention. The specification using only earlier information excludes paper counts, actor reach, and attention to nearby concerns from the target meeting. It uses only the corresponding quantities from the preceding meeting. Moving-block intervals resample blocks of one to four meetings, and an exact sign-flip test covers all $2^{19}$ paired sign assignments. A check using later information replaces predictors for the target meeting with the next meeting's attention for the 18 meetings with available data. We also rebuild the analysis for ATCMs 29--45 from the January 2024 single-category snapshot and compare it with the current multi-label reconstruction over the same meetings. \path{scripts/adversarial_forecast_checks.py} and \path{scripts/audit_snapshot_nowcast_sensitivity.py} reproduce these tests.

\subsection{Auditing formal citations among Measures}\label{sec:formal-inheritance-audit}
The prediction asks whether attention helps locate adopted output. A separate audit asks what formal record later Measures cite. We froze the adopted body text from the official detail page of every one of the 277 Measures on 16 August 2026. The pinned JSON Lines (JSONL) corpus records each official URL, retrieval date, extraction status, and body hash; an adjacent manifest records the corpus SHA-256 and verifies the source inventory and all 277 records. The audit consumes this corpus without live requests. We extract exact references of the form \emph{Measure}, \emph{Decision}, or \emph{Resolution} followed by a number and year. Instrument numbers restart each year, so type, number, and year jointly identify every source. A reference counts only when it resolves to an earlier entry in the pinned official inventory. Repeated mentions of the same source within one Measure count as one link.

The audit reports the number of later Measures citing each source type, the number of distinct earlier instruments cited, and the number of source--Measure links. A later Measure can cite several source types, so the percentages in \cref{fig:selective-translation}b overlap. These exact references provide a conservative record of formal citation. They do not establish that the earlier instrument caused the later one, and a policy sequence can omit an intermediate source from its final adopted body text. We also screen Measure titles for ``management plan'' and ``revised management plan'' to describe the recurring content of the Measure record.

To examine whether heavily discussed Resolutions later receive a citation from a Measure, we retain Resolutions with ten subsequent regular meetings in the archive. A Resolution enters the linked set only when a Measure adopted within those ten meetings contains an exact year-qualified citation to it. For each linked Resolution, we compare same-category paper attention with uncited Resolutions sharing at least one official category and report its percentile within that set. We count time in meetings rather than calendar years. The linked sample is small, so this is a descriptive boundary test rather than a fitted transition model. \path{scripts/audit_resolution_measure_ladders.py} and \path{scripts/analyze_resolution_hardening_attention.py} reproduce the audit.

\subsection{Paper--output associations}\label{sec:attention-output-models}
The category--meeting analysis orders the record by ATCM number, so one step is the immediately preceding meeting even when more than one calendar year elapsed. For broad category $i$ at ATCM $t$, we measure three earlier histories: fractionally counted papers in $i$; papers in its five nearest broad categories, weighted using the map available before each source meeting; and adopted outputs in $i$. For the primary one-meeting analysis, each history comes from ATCM $t-1$, and its nearby-paper weights use the map built through $t-2$. We add one and take the logarithm.

A Poisson pseudo-maximum-likelihood model uses category and meeting fixed effects. The reported rate ratios give the change associated with doubling one plus an earlier count. We cluster covariance by category and meeting. Because there are only 15 category clusters, the intervals require caution. They also condition on the author-coded hierarchy and estimated map; the models do not propagate uncertainty in those objects.

We repeat the analysis after combining the preceding one, two, three, five, eight, or ten meetings. The available meeting set therefore shrinks as the window grows. To separate window length from sample period, we repeat every horizon on the 19 meetings shared by the ten-meeting model (ATCMs 29--47). Composition checks exclude General matters, Area protection and management, or both. The latter matters because recurring area and management-plan work makes up almost half of the adopted-output category weight. All comparisons describe temporal association between documentary streams and adopted outputs, not a causal effect of papers on output. \path{scripts/analyze_output_category_families.py} reproduces the models.

\subsection{The paper--output association changes with sample period}\label{sec:attention-accumulation-results}

\subsection{A boundary test: the 1991 Madrid Protocol}\label{sec:madrid-shock}

To ask whether pre-shock position also predicts responses outside routine portfolio evolution, we examine the reorientation surrounding the 1991 Madrid Protocol. Parties adopted the Convention on the Regulation of Antarctic Mineral Resource Activities (CRAMRA) in Wellington in 1988 and abandoned it after Australia and France declined to sign it in 1989; the Protocol negotiations of 1989--91 replaced it \cite{Joyner1998}. The documentary record in 1986--90 therefore contains attention to the failed instrument as well as the concerns later reorganized by the Protocol.

The design uses pre-1991 inputs where possible and defines the boosted set as the eight concerns whose archive share grew most between 1986--90 and 1992--96. We restrict this set to concerns with at least $1\%$ pre-presence, excluding categories that the Protocol itself created. Using documents from 1961--90, we measure how close an actor's pre-1991 portfolio ($RPA \ge 1$) lies to the boosted set by its highest shared-specialization score. The outcome measures the share of an actor's papers in each 5-year window that falls in the boosted set. We model it with window dummies interacted with this closeness measure, actor and window fixed effects, window-by-portfolio-size controls, and actor-clustered standard errors. The model covers 30 actors and 263 actor-window observations with at least three papers.

The pre-period coefficients do not reject parallel trends (joint $F(5,\cdot)=1.61$, $p=0.159$), although this low-powered diagnostic does not establish that the shock is exogenous. Every post-shock closeness-by-window coefficient is negative and small relative to its uncertainty (range $-0.83$ to $-0.19$ percentage points). A second-pass model asks whether closeness to the Protocol's compliance set predicts entry into that set; its coefficient is also negative and uncertain ($-3.02$, SE $3.12$, $p=0.334$). The closeness measure correlates strongly with pre-1991 portfolio size ($r=0.87$), which the window-by-portfolio-size controls absorb.

The negative, imprecise estimates leave the association under an unusually large institutional reorientation unresolved. Pre-shock position did not clearly distinguish who responded, and the design cannot determine whether the collective response overrode a normally local process or arose through a different process altogether. We report this exploratory boundary test because its outcome limits the mechanism reading of the main text. \path{scripts/madrid_shock_exploration.py} reproduces the analysis.
